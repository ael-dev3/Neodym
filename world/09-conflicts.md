# 09 — Central Conflicts and Moral Complexity

## Major conflicts

### Humans versus Aurelith

The central conflict is not only revolt against foreign rule. It is a struggle over who gets to define survival. The Aurelith believe humanity must be protected from its own catastrophic freedom. Humans increasingly believe protection has become spiritual captivity.

### Aurelith versus Aurelith

Hardliners, reformers, archivists, prophecy engineers, dissidents, isolationists, and Final Clarifiers disagree over humanity's future. Some want gradual autonomy. Some want deeper rule. Some want to abandon humans. Some want to transform them into something more manageable.

### Humans versus humans

Collaborators, bread loyalists, rebels, zealots, nobles, workers, and black-market profiteers all have conflicting interests. Human liberation can be betrayed by revenge, class ambition, religious fanaticism, or hunger.

### Other races choosing sides

Cairnkin want mine freedom but fear economic collapse. Vharakul honor treaties that keep their children safe. Thallor resist disclosure. Nacren profit from ambiguity. Eirrem want truth but may seek vengeance. Dravyr survival could destroy entire cities.

### Religious conflict

The Doctrine of Continuance frames obedience as sacred. The Laughing Flame frames change as holy. The Ash Synod frames destruction of Neodym as salvation. Still Cantors and rebel prophets may both be sincere.

### Class conflict

Sealed Houses enjoy comfort under occupation. Workers bear the costs of infrastructure. Registered citizens fear falling into unsealed status. Rebels must decide whether to punish collaborators or win them.

### Generational conflict

Older humans remember famine and are cautious. Younger humans see only cages. Older Aurelith fear repeating The Sky Fever. Younger Clarified Aurelith inherit memories of disasters they never personally experienced and may question them.

### Philosophical conflict

- Is peace without agency a form of death?
- Is freedom meaningful if it predictably leads to mass suffering?
- Can a ruler be benevolent while denying consent?
- Does mortality make humans foolish or morally urgent?
- Does perfect memory prevent wisdom by preventing forgiveness?

## Structural risks

- Destroying the Mirror Choir may also destroy plague wards and food distribution.
- Killing Aurelith leaders may empower worse Aurelith factions.
- Human victory may become a retaliatory human empire.
- Outside powers, monsters, or Dravyr remnants may exploit civil war.
- The Concord may be suppressing something genuinely worse beneath the world.
- Neodym itself may be awakening and using both sides.

## Moral complexity notes

### Were the Aurelith ever right to intervene?

Yes, at least partly. The Sky Fever or a similar catastrophe was real enough that millions died and many survivors begged for guidance. The Aurelith saved lives, restored infrastructure, and prevented immediate extinction in several regions.

### Did humans cause a catastrophe?

Humans helped cause one, but they were not alone. Cairnkin refinement, Dravyr fire, Aurelith secrecy, and Neodym itself all played roles. The official story concentrates guilt where it is politically useful.

### Is freedom worth chaos?

The world should never answer this cheaply. Freedom is worth risk, but not all risks are noble. A rebellion that burns hospitals and granaries has already begun to imitate the cruelty it hates.

### Can immortal minds understand mortal life?

Only with humility. Some Aurelith can love mortals, but their long lives tempt them to treat human grief as weather. The best among them know this is a moral disability.

### Can humans survive without oppression?

Not by simply tearing everything down. They need institutions, memory practices, food systems, medicine, and law that are accountable rather than custodial.

### Can the Aurelith change?

Some can. The harder question is whether immortal institutions can change before crisis makes reform impossible.

### Is coexistence possible?

Yes, if memory becomes shared rather than owned, prophecy becomes counsel rather than command, and human provinces gain real power to consent, refuse, and remember.

### What would a bad human victory look like?

A purified human state that executes collaborators, enslaves Aurelith remnants, destroys Neodym infrastructure, unleashes famine, and calls revenge liberation.

### What would a good compromise look like?

A federated order where archive access is public, identity seals are voluntary or limited by law, human self-rule is real, Aurelith expertise remains available, and other races receive reparations and sovereignty.

### What truth would make both sides question themselves?

The Sky Fever was caused by an attempted union of human Bloodsong and Aurelith Neodym craft meant to save the world from an older threat. Both sides turned a failed collaboration into a myth of unilateral guilt.

### What each side refuses to admit

- **Aurelith:** they need human mortality to keep their own civilization spiritually alive.
- **Humans:** some forms of order they resent are the only reason their children are alive.
- **Rebels:** liberation requires governance, not just courage.
- **Collaborators:** comfort bought with another person's silence is still captivity.

## Visual notes

- Conflict imagery should avoid simple good-versus-evil framing: rebels saving a child from a plague ward they also sabotage; an Aurelith executioner weeping silently; collaborators feeding refugees while naming them to the census.
- Use silver versus ember as a recurring contrast, but allow mixed palettes for morally ambiguous scenes.

## Open questions

- What older threat might justify some Aurelith secrecy?
- Which rebel faction is most likely to become tyrannical?
- Which Aurelith faction could plausibly negotiate?
- What institution should survive the revolution in transformed form?
