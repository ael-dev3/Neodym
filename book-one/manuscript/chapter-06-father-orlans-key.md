# Chapter 6 — Father Orlan's Key

The court bell was counting noon when Elsbet reached Saint Brenna's door.

By the tenth stroke Wren stumbled on the chapel step and caught herself against the saint carved into the lintel. Mud ran from both of them in strings. Rain had soaked the copied slat against Elsbet's wrist. The tally board under Wren's shawl knocked once against the doorpost, a small hollow sound.

Father Orlan had barred the chapel door from inside.

"It is us," Elsbet called.

The bar lifted after a pause too long for welcome. Orlan opened the door only as far as his shoulder, saw Wren, then saw the mud on Elsbet's skirt and the brown water dripping from her cuffs. His face tightened before he could hide it.

"What did you do?"

"Saw what was already done."

"That is not an answer."

"No," Elsbet said, pushing gently past him before rain made her tell the truth badly. "It is what answers look like when powerful men get there first."

The chapel was dim and close after the open bank. Father Orlan had shuttered the side windows. The rough register lay under linen beside the unsealed fair copy, its blankness more accusing than ink. The brass key hung from Orlan's belt cord, dark where his fingers had worried it.

Wren stayed near the door.

"Come in," Orlan said.

"So you can bar it behind me?"

He flinched. "So rain does not ruin whatever you have brought."

That made Wren's chin lift. "How did you know I brought anything?"

"Because children who have been counted out of houses do not come back empty-handed."

For once she had no fast answer.

Elsbet drew the copied candle slat from her sleeve and laid it on the table. The blue wax had blurred but held. Eight close notches. One apart. River line. Joined pair. Farthing dot.

"Matra cut it in front of us from her fever-season slat," Elsbet said. "She keeps the old one. She will not come unless called. Ruan sent for court candles and warned her accounts would be reviewed."

Father Orlan did not touch the slat. He bent close, squinting until his nose nearly shadowed it.

"This will be called kindling."

"Then let him call it kindling in front of Matra Voss."

"If she comes."

"If she is called."

"By whom?" His voice sharpened, then broke smaller. "By me? By a priest who cannot swear whether he reads a nine or a smudge without a girl to tell him?"

Wren crossed the room then. She pulled the wrapped tally board from under her shawl and set it beside the slat with more care than she had shown the chapel door.

"My mother's crossing board," she said. "Master Kest's woman did not find where the cord was hid, but she counted the house. Ruan's men were tying manor rope to our post before court. Elsbet saw. I saw. They saw us seeing."

The old priest sank onto his stool as if each word had added weight to his knees.

"They saw you at the bank?"

"They spoke to us."

"Brenna keep us."

"She may start any time," Wren said.

Elsbet expected Orlan to rebuke her. He only closed his eyes.

A second bell began outside, not the court bell now but a handbell moving down the lane. One strike. A pause. One strike again. Summons rhythm. People would be counting whose trouble had found legs.

Father Orlan opened his eyes and took the key from his belt.

"No," Wren said at once.

He stopped with the key halfway to the chest lock. "No?"

"You put things in boxes and then men say no one can see them."

"If these things stay in wet sleeves and frightened hands, men will call them planted, altered, stolen, or taught after the quarrel began." He held up the key. It trembled, but his voice did not. "Custody matters. I hate it. It matters."

Elsbet hated that he was right.

"The cord stays with me," Wren said.

"Yes," Elsbet said before Orlan could answer. "The cord is hers. The slat copy and tally board can be described and kept under chapel key until court asks. Not hidden. Kept."

Wren looked at her, searching for the wool on the knife.

"If he locks them away," she said, "how do I know he opens it again?"

Father Orlan put the key flat on the table between them.

It was ordinary: brass worn pale along the teeth, the bow darkened by sweat and candle soot. Elsbet had seen it a thousand times and never counted how much of Harrowick Ford lived behind it: baptisms, shames, promises, tithes, debts, dead women, late fees, names children needed adults to remember.

"Because if I do not," he said, "you will tell the court I locked your mother's name in a box."

Wren stared at him.

"Would that help?"

"It would ruin me."

"Good."

"Yes," he said, and the word cost him.

He picked up the key again and opened the chest.

The smell came out first: old leather, iron, cold wax, damp paper. Orlan lifted the rough register beside the fair copy. Under it lay seal cords, blank scraps, a cracked horn box of sand, and a folded paper Elsbet had not seen before. He saw her notice it.

"There," he said quietly. "Since every lock is opening today."

He unfolded the paper.

Elsbet recognized Ruan's neat clerk hand by the way it made mercy look accounted for. Roof lead after the All Saints wind. Fever burial wax. Two pauper coffins. Lime for the west wall. Sixteen shillings, three pence, and a quarter. Secured against chapel candle dues and copy fees. Reviewable at flood court if arrears persisted.

At the bottom, Father Orlan's seal mark sat in dull brown wax.

Wren leaned over it. "You owe him?"

"I owed the dead dry roof and candles," Orlan said. "The poor box was empty. The roof had opened over the register chest. Three bodies waited because their families had no wax and the ground was iron. Ruan said the manor would advance coin until Easter dues came in. Easter dues did not come in. Fever takes work before it takes breath."

Elsbet read the amount again. Sixteen shillings could bind a chapel as neatly as Tomas's smaller bond bound a household.

"That is why you said unclear," she said.

"Partly." He looked at the rough book instead of at her. "If Ruan says I borrowed under seal and cannot repay, Harl Brey can put the chapel seal under manor keeping. If he says my eyes made false entries, the same. Then births, deaths, marriages, candles, all of it goes through the manor room before it becomes anything. Poor people will still stand at this door. Their names will leave by his."

"So Wren pays to keep everyone else's names possible."

Orlan's mouth worked once. "That is the sin as I have dressed it, yes."

The handbell struck outside again, closer.

Elsbet wanted anger. It would have been clean. Instead she felt the shape of his fear and knew why it had fitted him. A chapel could be stolen without burning. A child could be stolen without dragging. A lie could be made lawful by giving every frightened person one good reason to step aside.

"Then do not make us spend her," she said.

He folded the debt paper and put it back in the chest, but he did not close the lid.

"I cannot win this for her."

"I did not ask you to win."

"You will. Before day ends, you will look at me as if a key is a sword I was too cowardly to lift. It is not. It opens a box. That is all."

Wren touched the wrapped tally board with one finger. "Then open it where they can see."

The handbell stopped at the chapel gate.

Father Orlan stood. His hand went to the key, then to the rough register, then to the unsealed fair copy. Elsbet saw the order of his fear and what he might still do.

A fist struck the door.

"From the flood court," a man's voice called. Not a boy this time. The broad manor man from the ferry bank. "Summons for Father Orlan Rusk, Elsbet Vale, and the girl in dispute."

Wren's face emptied around the last words.

Orlan did not move.

Elsbet went to the door before stillness could become refusal. She lifted the bar and opened it a hand's width. The broad man stood under the porch, rain shining on his beard. He held out a folded paper sealed with Lady Merrow's hawk. Mud marked his boots to the calf. Ferry-bank mud.

"Master Kest says the steward will hear the wardship first," he said. "Since the chapel has made itself interested."

Elsbet took the paper. "The steward says, or Master Kest says?"

His smile was small. "Read it with those useful eyes."

She broke the seal because it was addressed to the chapel and because delay would not make it kinder.

The summons was short: Harl Brey's name at the top, Ruan's hand beneath. Father Orlan to attend with fair copy or cause for failure. Elsbet Vale as copyist witness. Wren Reed, called Dain, minor female, to be produced for placement pending cottage, meadow, and ferry claim.

Wren Reed, called Dain.

Elsbet read the line once, then again, because rage could misread and she would not give Ruan that gift.

"Well?" the broad man said.

Father Orlan came to stand behind her. He had the chest key in one hand and the rough register under his other arm, wrapped in its linen.

"Tell Master Kest," he said, voice thin but carrying, "that Saint Brenna's register will answer under my key, not his hurry."

The man's eyes dropped to the covered book. For the first time since the meadow, he looked uncertain.

Only a little. It was enough to see the shape of the risk.

"Court bell has rung," he said.

"Then we should not waste it," Elsbet answered, and shut the door.

Inside, Wren had taken up the marriage cord. The blue and yellow knots lay across her palm like something alive and hurt.

"They wrote Reed," she said.

"I saw."

"They wrote called Dain. As if my name is a rumor."

Elsbet folded the summons and slipped it beside Tomas's bond strip. Threat beside threat. Record beside record.

Father Orlan locked the slat copy and tally board in the chest, then put the key back on his belt. He did not hand it to Elsbet. He only took the unsealed fair copy and tucked it under the register cloth.

"At court," he said, "do not argue about whether Ruan is cruel. Cruelty is not a form error. Count what can be counted. Read what can be read. Make them choose the lie where everyone hears it."

Wren wound the cord around her wrist once.

"And if they still choose it?"

No saint answered. No bell. Only rain on the roof, and the old priest's key knocking softly against his thigh.

Elsbet opened the chapel door.

"Then they do not get to call it clean," she said, and stepped out toward court.
