# Chapter 12 — The Name in the Margin

The margin was narrower than Elsbet remembered.

All day the blank beside Maura's death line had seemed large enough to hold fear, duty, a child's whole inheritance, and every careful word Ruan Kest had tried to teach the room to swallow. Now, with the fair copy laid flat under Saint Brenna's smoking lamp, it was only a finger's width of paper between a clean entry and the page edge. Too much pressure would blot it. Too little would look like apology.

Father Orlan stood beside the board with Harl Brey's order strip open in both hands.

"Read it again," Wren said.

She had asked three times already. No one told her to stop.

Orlan bent toward the wet-sanded ink. "Copyist Vale to prepare margin cause in the fair-copy hand. No seal before review. Name disputed in open court."

"Not decided," Wren said.

"No," Elsbet said. "Not decided."

The funeral pall lay folded near the brazier where Wren had refused to sleep under it until the paper was done. Mud had dried along the hem of her skirt. The marriage cord hung from her wrist, blue and yellow dark against skin rubbed red by rain and old knots. She watched the fair copy as if it might try to crawl away.

Elsbet trimmed her own pen, not the court pen. Her knife rasped the quill. A shaving curled onto the board.

"A margin cause is not a judgment," Father Orlan said.

"I know."

"It cannot grant the meadow. It cannot settle wardship. It cannot make Edric Dain less absent or Master Kest less skilled at waiting."

"I know."

"Then write only what can stand."

Elsbet looked at him. The words belonged to Ruan first and to her now by theft or penance. Orlan's face had gone hollow with the day's courage. It had not made him grand. It had made him look like an old man who had finally discovered that a key was heavy.

"Bring the rough book nearer," she said.

He did. Not into her hands. Beside them. Custody mattered. She had hated that sentence when he said it, and hated it more because it was true. The rough register opened with a sigh of damp leather. Its pages lifted at the corners like old wounds resisting cloth.

Maura Reed joined by cord and witness to Edric Dain.

Maura Dain. Winter fever. Nine lights. One child. Ferry bank.

Two lines. Years apart. Brown ink, cramped hand, grief, fees, weather, and no miracle in any of it. Elsbet set the fair copy so that the death entry waited beneath her hand. In the fair line she had already written what no one disputed: Maura, winter fever, one child. Ruan had wanted no husband entered. Harl would not yet let her write wife with a seal behind it. The margin would have to carry the truth without pretending it weighed more than paper could bear.

A knock came at the chapel door.

Wren snatched the cord against her chest. Father Orlan's hand went to his key. Elsbet kept the pen above the ink, not touching.

"Court order," Matra Voss called from outside. "And wet feet. Open before both worsen."

Orlan lifted the bar.

Matra entered first with rain on her shoulders and a small horn-latched box hugged to her ribs. Behind her came the manor boy who had carried court candles, trying to look official while water ran from his cap into his eyes. He held a folded order under one arm and a witness taper under the other.

"I brought the old slat," Matra said. "I did not bring permission to have it stolen."

"No one will take it," Orlan said.

"People say that with locks in the room." Matra's eyes went to Wren. Softer, she said, "Come see before I shut the lid again."

They gathered around the chapel chest because truth, Elsbet thought, had become a thing that needed people watching how it travelled. Orlan unlocked the chest and laid out the copied slat, Wren's wrapped household tally, and the rough register cloth. Matra opened her horn box. Inside lay three old fever slats tied with black thread. She touched one, then another, then lifted the third.

The old slat was darker than its copy, stained by smoke and hand grease and winter. Eight close notches. One apart. River line. Joined pair. Farthing dot. Blue wax rubbed into the end so thinly it looked less like color than memory.

The manor boy leaned close.

"Do not breathe law on my wood," Matra said.

He straightened at once.

Elsbet compared the old slat to the copy under the lamp. The copy was faithful. Not equal. Faithful. That difference mattered enough to hurt.

"It matches," she said. "Eight chapel lights and one household. Maura Dain. Fever burial. Fee short by one farthing. Ferry token against thaw."

Matra's jaw tightened at hearing her private marks turned into court words, but she nodded. "That is what I cut before anyone thought profit could be made from forgetting."

Wren reached toward the slat and stopped herself. "You saw the cord."

"I saw a blue-yellow cord laid for the household light. I saw your mother buried from the ferry bank. I did not see the court become honest while I was young enough to believe in easy things. Do not make my slat say more."

"I won't," Wren said, though she was looking at Elsbet when she promised.

Orlan locked the copied slat and tally back in the chest after the boy had seen them. Matra kept the original in her horn box. No one liked the arrangement. That was why it seemed likely to survive until morning.

The manor boy offered his folded order. "Master Brey's clerk says the margin cause should name what was produced before evening bell. He says if it runs too long, he will charge extra sand."

Matra snorted. "Charge him for breathing. He does it badly."

Elsbet took the order, read it, and set it beside Tomas's bond strip.

The bond strip had gone soft from rain and skin heat. Household conduct to be considered. Review pending. It had spent the day pressed against Maura's proof as if threats and records were kin. Tomas waited under the mill eave, split-lipped and out of reach because a touch might tempt breach.

Wren saw where Elsbet looked.

"Will your brother be safe if you write this?"

Elsbet could have lied. The lie stood ready, kinder than truth and just as useless.

"No," she said. "He will be less safe in some ways. Safer in none I can promise."

Wren's mouth trembled once. She held it still by force. "Then why keep writing?"

Because Maura was dead and Tomas was alive, and neither fact excused spending the other.

Elsbet dipped the pen.

"Because if fear chooses all our words," she said, "Ruan need not write anything himself."

She set the nib in the margin.

The first letter wanted to spread. She held her breath until the ink settled into the paper.

Cause entered before seal by order of flood court: rough chapel register records Maura Reed joined by cord and witness to Edric Dain; death entry records Maura Dain, nine lights, one child, ferry bank.

She stopped, sanded lightly, waited. Wren leaned so close Matra caught the back of her shawl.

"Let wet ink be wet," the candlewright said. "Even courage smears."

Elsbet wrote the second line smaller.

Candlewright Matra Voss produced fever-season slat before evening bell, marking eight chapel lights and one household under Maura Dain; chapel-held copy slat and ferry tally remain under key pending review.

Her hand began to ache. The margin narrowed further. Everyone read over her fingers: Orlan fearing too much, Matra too little, Wren wanting one word to become a door, the manor boy storing phrases for men who would use them poorly.

She wrote the last line.

Name disputed in open court. No omission of husband line, wardship transfer, or new ferry lease to be sealed from this fair copy before review.

The room held still.

Elsbet lifted the pen.

No bell rang. No saint moved. No old road answered from the wet dark. The ink was only ink, black and slightly uneven where her hand had tired. It did not look powerful. That was another truth about records. They looked weakest at the moment they began binding people.

"Read it," Wren whispered.

Elsbet read it aloud.

At Maura Dain, Wren shut her eyes. Not long. Long enough for the name to enter and find somewhere to sit.

"Does that save her?" the child asked.

No one hurried to answer this time.

"It keeps the fair copy from pretending she was never Dain," Elsbet said. "It tells the next hand where the knife is. It gives Harl Brey less room to look away, and Ruan less clean paper."

"But it does not save her."

"Not all the way."

Wren nodded. She had become expert in fractions of safety. Elsbet hated every adult who had taught her, including herself.

Father Orlan sanded the margin with a pinch so careful it looked like prayer. When the ink dulled, he set the fair copy in the open chest, not beneath the rough register and not above it. Beside it. Then he placed his key on the lid and looked at Wren.

"Under her name," he said.

Wren put one finger on the lid before he closed it. "Under her name."

The key turned.

Outside, the ferry bell sounded once from the river: not alarm, only crossing. Old tolls. Court pouch. Men pulling rope because water did not care who was right. Farther off, the mill stones began night grinding: stone over grain, debt over body, the world still asking payment.

Matra tied her horn box shut. "I will sleep with this under my ribs and a knife under the pillow. If anyone asks, I learned piety from Saint Brenna."

The manor boy looked unsure whether to report that.

"Report what you saw," Elsbet told him.

He glanced at the chest, the order strip, Wren's cord, Matra's box, Orlan's key. For once a messenger seemed to understand that seeing was work.

"I saw the margin written before seal," he said.

"Good," Orlan said. "Carry that cleanly."

When they had gone, Wren finally lay down beside the brazier. She did not use the funeral pall as a blanket. She folded it once and set it between herself and the chest, a wall made of mourning cloth. The cord stayed on her wrist. Her eyes remained open.

Elsbet went to the chapel door.

Rain had thinned to mist. Across the village, one cart lantern moved toward the ferry, swinging low. Beyond the ridge, where the Glass Mile lay, a pale seam showed through cloud and distance, dry-looking or merely imagined so by tired eyes. It gave no sign. It named no heir. It did not bend down to shame Ruan, comfort Wren, free Tomas, or teach a court how to read.

Human roads bent around it all the same.

Elsbet looked back at the chest, at the leaking roof, at Wren's small shape keeping watch over a locked name.

Tomorrow the fair copy would travel again. Ruan would find another clean phrase, Tomas would lift sacks under mercy counted as debt, and Harl Brey would decide how much dirt a roll could bear.

Tonight, Maura Dain was written in the margin where absence had been ordered to sit.

It was not enough.

It would have to begin there.
