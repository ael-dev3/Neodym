# Chapter 4 — Matra's Wax Slats

First light came gray and cold, showing every place the night had failed to dry: Wren's skirt, Tomas's boots, the fair copy waiting clean on Elsbet's board. Father Orlan slept in his chair with one hand on the covered register and the other wrapped around his key cord.

Elsbet did not wake him until she had folded the fair copy into its cloth.

His eyes opened at once. Fear had been waiting closer than sleep.

"Do not take the rough book," he said.

"I am not a fool."

"Frightened people become inventive fools."

"Then keep me dull. The register stays with you. The copy stays unsealed. Wren and I go to Matra Voss."

Tomas rose from the step where he had spent the last hour pretending not to shiver. "I am coming."

"You are going to the mill. Tell Miller you know nothing. It is the safest true thing you can say."

He hated that, but the debt strip in Elsbet's sleeve had already said more than comfort could. Wren watched while grown people weighed one danger against another and called it sense.

Father Orlan rubbed his face. "Matra will not thank you."

"No," Elsbet said. "But if her slats remember what everyone else fears to, she should know before Ruan does."

The lane behind Saint Brenna's chapel was all ruts and cold water. Elsbet took it instead of the front road, where manor boys liked to be seen carrying warnings. Wren walked beside her, the marriage cord hidden beneath her shawl.

"If she says no," Wren said, "do we steal it?"

"No."

"You answered fast."

"Because stealing proof makes it bad proof."

"Bad proof is better than no proof."

"Only until Ruan asks how we got it. Then it becomes a rope around our necks and Matra's." Elsbet stepped over a runnel carrying straw downhill. "We ask. If she refuses, we find a way to make refusal visible."

"That sounds like no."

"It is no with shoes on."

Wren snorted despite herself. The sound vanished almost at once.

Matra Voss's candle shed stood behind her cottage, close enough to the lane for buyers to smell rendered fat, beeswax scrap, smoke, damp twine, and bitter herbs before they saw the door. Light showed beneath it. Matra began before dawn because wax hardened when it pleased itself.

Elsbet knocked once.

"If you have come for cheap tapers, wait for bell," Matra called.

"It is Elsbet Vale."

The scraping inside stopped.

Wren lifted her chin. "And Wren Dain."

For a moment the shed held its breath. Then the bolt drew back.

Matra Voss filled the doorway with a dip-stick in one hand and a linen apron stiff with old wax. She was square-shouldered, gray-haired, bare-armed despite the cold, a burn scar crossing one wrist. Her eyes went first to Elsbet, then to Wren's hidden fist.

"No," she said.

"You have not heard what I am asking."

"I heard enough last winter when Maura Dain died with nine lights and Master Kest's clerk asked why widows were growing expensive." Matra looked up the lane before stepping aside. "In, then. If you must ruin me, do not do it in the rain."

The shed was warmer than the chapel, but not kinder. Candle rods hung from rafters. Wax pans sat over banked coals. Thin slats dangled from pegs by the workbench, bundled in red, blue, or undyed cord and covered with notches, knife crosses, soot rubs, pinholes, and colored wax. To a court clerk they would look like kindling. To Matra they were a ledger.

Wren stared. "Which one is Mother's?"

"Do not touch." Matra's voice cracked like a stick. Softer, she added, "Please."

Wren pulled her hand back.

Elsbet shut the door. "Ruan moved court to first bell. Father Orlan delayed the seal last night."

Matra's face closed. "Old fool."

"For once, not entirely. The rough register has Maura joined to Edric Dain. The death line has nine candles. Wren has the cord. We need your slat to show the candle count was bought and recorded under Maura's married name."

"Need," Matra said. She dipped two joined wicks into a pan and lifted them slowly. Wax skinned over in cloudy layers. "People use that word when they have found the person who pays."

Wren stepped forward. "My mother paid you."

Matra's hand tightened on the rod. Drops of wax fell and hardened on the floor. "Your mother paid half. The fever paid the rest by leaving me three households who could not pay at all. I set lights because bodies should not go dark from arithmetic. Do not make that face at me, child. I know what I did."

"Then say it."

"To whom? A steward who buys court candles by the dozen? A bailiff who can decide next winter that manor wax comes from Millcross instead? I have two vats still owed for. If the contract goes, my cottage goes with it. Widows do not keep roofs by being noble in other people's stories."

Wren's eyes shone, but no tear fell. "My mother was a widow."

"I know. That is why I remember."

A knock struck the cottage door beyond the shed wall.

All three froze.

Matra blew out the nearest lamp with two fingers pinched around the flame. "Back," she whispered.

Elsbet drew Wren behind a rack of dipped tapers. Through a crack between boards she saw a manor boy in a dark cap holding a folded chit under his cloak.

Matra wiped her hands on her apron and went through the inner door.

"Mistress Voss," the boy said when she opened. "From Master Kest. Court candles to be delivered by noon. Twelve bench lights, two seal candles, one witness taper."

"Court was first bell."

"Now noon. Steward's cart stuck at Moat Lane." The boy held out the chit. "Master Kest says accounts will be reviewed with the candle contract after court. All trade slats to be clean if called."

Matra did not take the paper at once.

"He said you would know what clean means," the boy added.

Matra took the chit. "Tell Master Kest I sell candles, not miracles. If he wants clean, he can keep mud from men's boots."

The boy blinked, unsure whether he had been insulted on behalf of power. "Noon," he said.

"Then do not stand in my working light."

She shut the door in his face.

When she came back, her color had gone flat. She held the chit as if paper could burn.

"Now you see the price," she said.

Elsbet stepped from behind the rack. "I see him naming it. What does he fear in your slats?"

Matra laughed once, ugly and honest. "What every man with a clean paper fears. A dirty little mark made before he thought to forbid it."

She went to the workbench and pulled down a bundle tied with black thread. Fever season. Elsbet knew it before Matra said so. The slats were darker than the others, handled too often or smoked too close. Matra sorted by touch, thumb moving along notches.

"Brenna take my memory," she muttered. "Reed, boy, no kin. Hask, six poor. Tallow for the twins. Dain."

Wren made a sound so small it might have been the shed settling.

Matra laid one slat on the table.

It was no longer than Elsbet's hand. One side bore eight close notches and a ninth set apart, deeper and angled. Blue wax smeared the end. Beside it, in Matra's shorthand, were two small marks: a river line and a joined pair.

"Read it," Wren demanded.

"It is not writing," Matra said.

"Read it anyway."

Matra stared at the slat. "Maura Dain. Fever burial. Eight chapel lights, one household. Blue-yellow cord seen. Fee short one farthing. Ferry token left against thaw."

Elsbet's throat tightened. "The joined mark is the cord?"

"The joined mark means I saw why the ninth was set. It does not mean I married them. It does not mean I know whether a court clerk will pretend not to understand wood."

"Will you come?" Wren asked.

Matra did not look at her. "No."

The word was quieter than the first no.

Wren stepped back as if struck.

Matra gripped the table edge. "If I stand in that court without summons, Ruan says I stirred a disputed child for spite. If I leave the shed, he says the court candles were late because I chose gossip over duty. If my contract goes, no one feeds me for remembering your mother. Praise is thin soup."

"Then keep it," Wren said. "Keep your roof."

She reached for the door.

Elsbet caught her sleeve, not hard enough to hold if Wren truly pulled. "Wait." She looked at the slat, then at Matra's closed face. "Cut a tally copy. Match the old slat in front of us. You keep the original bundle. I carry the copy and say where it was cut. If court calls you, you answer only what you must."

Matra stared at her.

It was not a clean answer. A copy slat was weaker than an original, easy to mock as prepared for quarrel. But the old slat would stay in Matra's custody, and the copy would force the question without handing Ruan the whole trade ledger.

"That is thin," Matra said.

"So are poor people's bridges. We cross until they break."

Wren wiped her nose with the back of her hand, angry at the need for wiping. "Would it help?"

"Enough to be dangerous," Elsbet said.

Matra looked from Wren to the old slat, then toward the cottage door where Ruan's message had come. Her scarred wrist flexed once. At last she took a blank slat from a peg and set it beside the old one.

"Watch," she said. "Both of you. If I do this, you will not make my marks say more than they say."

Elsbet placed her ink-stained hand on the workbench. "I will say Matra Voss cut a copy from her fever-season candle slat. It marks Maura Dain's burial lights as eight chapel and one household. It does not by itself prove marriage."

Matra nodded once. "That oath may live."

She cut the marks slowly, each notch a small injury to the wood. Eight close. One apart. River line. Joined pair. Farthing dot. She warmed blue wax over the lamp, rubbed it into the end, then scraped most away so only a stain remained.

When she handed the copy slat to Wren, the child did not take it.

"To her," Wren said, nodding at Elsbet. "If I hold it, they will say I stole it."

Matra's mouth twisted. "You learn fast. I am sorry for it."

Elsbet took the slat. It weighed almost nothing. That was the terror of it. A strip of wood, a child's cord, an old priest's memory, and her own hand were meant to stand against a man with summons, debts, and a court bell.

Outside, noon's delay moved through the village in shouted fragments. Cart stuck. Court moved. Bailiff angry. River rising.

Matra returned the old slat to its bundle and tied the black thread hard. "Go by the back wash. The lane will have eyes now."

At the door, Wren turned. "You did not say no all the way."

Matra looked older in the gray light. "Most bravery in Harrowick Ford is not saying no all the way. Remember that before you judge us dead."

Wren nodded, but her face did not forgive.

Elsbet tucked the copy slat inside her sleeve beside Tomas's bond strip. Two thin records lay against her skin, one threatening her blood and one saving someone else's name.

By the back wash, water had climbed over the stepping stones. Elsbet went first so Wren could see where the mud held. Behind them, Matra's shed door bolted again.

Ahead, the court bell had not yet rung.

That was something.

Not mercy. Not safety. Something.
