# Chapter 1 - Levy Bell

Nera Brindle was scraping a boy's name off the levy roll when the bell began to ring.

The knife was a bread knife, too blunt for clean work and too sharp for honest excuses. It worried the parchment into pale curls while rain ticked against the shutters of the Split Loaf. Pell Quick's name had been written in Halven Marr's careful hand between two older boys: Perran Voss, seventeen, good with horses; Pell Quick, thirteen, no trade; Tobin Reed, sixteen, lame right foot.

No trade. As if hunger were not a trade. As if running messages, hauling flour, fetching eels from the ditch, holding horses for drunk men, and sleeping wherever no one kicked you awake did not count as work.

Nera shaved the ink until Pell's name became a bruise.

Osa Fen stood at the oven mouth with both arms white to the elbow in flour. She did not tell Nera to stop. That was how frightened she was.

"If they hold it to the window, they'll see," Osa said.

"Then don't let them hold it to the window."

"That's your plan? Keep Lord Venn's men away from windows?"

"I've had worse plans before breakfast."

Osa gave a sound that might have been a laugh if either of them had been younger. The ovens breathed behind her. Barley loaves swelled in the heat, their crusts splitting along the blessing cuts. Bread for market. Bread for widows. Bread now counted twice in Nera's head: once for mouths and once for what armed men could carry off.

The levy bell rang again from the bridge tower.

Not the market bell. Not the chapel bell for death. The levy bell had a flat iron note that made every house in Low Wicker remember it had sons, grain, and doors that could be broken.

"They're early," Osa said.

Nera blew parchment dust from the roll. Pell's name had not vanished. It had only become the kind of wound a clerk would notice if he wanted to.

"He is thirteen," she said.

"A pike doesn't ask."

Outside, boots splashed in the yard. Men shouted for the reeve. A horse struck the bakehouse wall hard enough to shake flour from the rafters. Nera folded the roll once, twice, and slid it under the cooling board.

Osa caught her wrist.

"If they find you with that scrape, they'll call it forgery. Not kindness. Not cleverness. Forgery."

"Then tell them I am bad with knives."

"Nera."

There was love in the warning, which made it worse. Osa had raised her on burnt crusts and practical fear after Nera's mother went into the ground with debts still being argued over her coffin. Osa believed survival was a craft. Bend early. Hide bread. Speak soft. Give men with banners a road wide enough to leave by.

Nera believed it too, most days.

Today Pell's name sat under a cooling board, and belief had become a poor tool.

The door banged open. Joryn Hale came in wet to the shoulders, spear in hand, local badge crooked on his coat. He was only twenty-six, but service had already taught his face to stand at attention before the rest of him knew why.

His eyes went to the table. Nera saw him see the knife.

"Roll," he said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Nera. Roll."

Osa moved between him and the cooling board with a peel in one hand. "You can wait like any man wanting bread."

"I'm not here for bread."

"Men never are when they mean to take it."

Joryn flinched. That was the trouble with decent men in bad coats. They still bled when struck, and then they did what the coat required.

"Osric Venn is at the bridge," he said. "Six riders. Two carts. Lord's seal. They want the names read in the yard."

"The reeve has the names," Nera said.

"The reeve is shaking too hard to read them."

A third bell note rolled over the roofs. Somewhere a dog began howling and was cuffed quiet.

Nera pulled the roll from under the board. Joryn saw the scraped place. His mouth tightened.

"Tell me that isn't what it looks like."

"It is parchment."

"Tell me."

"Pell is thirteen."

"Venn's men took a twelve-year-old from Dun Mere last spring because he could carry arrows."

"And did your badge feel heavy then?"

He looked away first. That had once meant something between them. When they were children, it meant Nera had won. Now it meant there were riders in the yard and winning was a word for songs.

"Hide him," Joryn said under his breath. "If he's not here when the roll is read, they may leave a summons instead of a rope."

"Where is he?"

Joryn's answer was cut off by a voice outside.

"Bring out the list, or bring out the man hiding it."

Osric Venn did not need to shout. He had one of those dry voices that made people lean in before they noticed they were obeying. Nera had known him all her life: miller's nephew, debt buyer, tithe counter, man who could remember a widow's missing half sack three winters later. He stood in the rain with a black cloak pinned by Lord Venn's bronze boar, his beard trimmed close, his boots clean because poorer men had walked the mud before him.

Behind him, two riders held the Bramble Gate banner low under the eaves.

Even they did not raise it toward the north road.

Beyond the village roofs, through the rain, Starvel Field lay like a paler piece of sky fallen onto the hill. Its white stones made a broken line above the old road. No sheep grazed there. No child fetched a ball there. No army crossed there, though every war map wished it could.

Nera had grown up with a dozen explanations and trusted none of them.

Osric followed her glance and smiled without warmth.

"Looking for elder mercy, Brindle? Wrong hill for that."

"I was looking at the weather."

"Weather doesn't save boys either."

He knew. Of course he knew. Men like Osric could smell a hidden name the way dogs smelled fat.

Halven Marr, reeve of Low Wicker, stood beside him bareheaded in the rain. His hands worried each other at his belt. A line of villagers had gathered along the yard wall: mothers with shawls over their hair, old men pretending they had only come for bread, boys trying to stand smaller than their bones allowed.

Pell was not among them.

Nera looked for him anyway, because worry had its own bad habits. She looked at the rain barrel where he hid stolen apples, the cart shed where he slept when Osa's attic was full, the low wall he walked whenever he wanted people to believe he had somewhere better to be. Nothing. No narrow face. No patched brown coat. No red wool tied around the cracked bell at his belt.

He had been eight when Nera first read him the only letter his mother left behind. He had asked her to read it again the next week, and the week after that, until Nera knew the words without looking. My brave little rat, it began, because his mother had been a woman with more love than poetry. Eat when they offer. Run when they don't. Trust Nera if she is still there.

Nera had been still there for five years. That was not law. That was not blood. That was only a promise made by showing up often enough that a child started sleeping easier.

Now a lord's man wanted to turn him into a pair of marching feet.

Nera held the roll tight enough to dent it.

Osric extended a gloved hand. "Read."

"By whose witness?"

A murmur went through the yard. Osa swore softly behind her.

Osric's smile widened. "By the witness of a burned tithe barn, three dead cart horses, and Lord Venn's right to call defense from every hearth under his road peace. If you want prettier words, ask your chapel woman to sew them on a cushion. Read."

Nera broke the seal thread. The roll opened with a damp sigh.

She read the older names first. Perran Voss. Tobin Reed. Harra's two sons, one of them coughing into his sleeve. Bran Alder, who had a baby due. Willo Teague, who stared at the mud as if it might swallow him kindly.

Each name changed the yard. Mothers stiffened. Fathers counted what grain could buy exemption and found the number too high. Boys tried to look brave because there were girls watching, and because shame worked faster than rope.

Then came the scraped place.

Nera could feel Joryn watching her.

She said, "Pell Quick, under age, no levy trade."

Osric's hand moved once. One of the riders stepped forward.

"That is not how Halven writes," Osric said.

The reeve shut his eyes.

Nera kept her voice level. "I am reading what is here."

"You are reading what you wish were there."

"Many men mistake those."

Osa made a strangled noise. Joryn whispered Nera's name, not warning now but grief preparing itself.

Before Osric could answer, something clattered in the lane beyond the yard. A boy ducked under the horse rail and stumbled into view, soaked to the skin, hair plastered flat, one cheek smeared black with ash.

Pell Quick held a dead man's boot against his chest.

For one stupid breath Nera thought only that Osa would murder him for tracking soot across the bread yard.

Then she saw the cut in the boot lining. The folded paper. The dark stain where rain had woken old blood.

Pell looked at the riders, at the roll in Nera's hand, and finally at her.

His mouth tried to make a joke and failed.

"I was at the tithe barn," he said. "Before sunup. I found this in the ash heap. Nera, it wasn't Ashmere men."

Every rider in the yard went still.

Osric took one step toward the boy.

Nera moved first. She crossed the mud, caught Pell by the shoulder, and pulled him behind her before anyone could call it sense.

The paper had already begun to open in his dirty hand. Nera saw six words written in a dying man's cramped script.

If I am found burned, look to Venn's own.

The rain came harder.

Across the yard, beneath the lowered banner, Osric Venn looked at Pell as if the levy had just become too small a punishment.
