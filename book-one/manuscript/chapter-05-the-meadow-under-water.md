# Chapter 5 — The Meadow Under Water

The back wash was not a road.

It was a ditch with opinions: where to suck a shoe, where to hide a stone, where to send brown water suddenly up to the knee as if the earth had opened a mouth. Matra's shed disappeared behind rain and willow scrub. The village sounds thinned to hammering water, cart wheels complaining somewhere beyond the houses, and Wren's breath held tight between her teeth.

Elsbet kept one hand inside her sleeve around the copied slat. The little strip of wood pressed against Tomas's bond paper. Proof and threat lay together, both too thin to warm her skin.

"This way floods first," Wren said.

"Matra said the lane had eyes."

"Ditches have eyes too. They are just poorer at telling."

Elsbet almost smiled. Then the mud took her left shoe to the ankle, and she had to grip a blackthorn stem to pull free.

Wren waited, not laughing. She had tucked the marriage cord back beneath her shawl. Every few steps her hand went to the hidden knot as if checking whether a name could fall out through cloth.

"We do not have to go to the meadow," Elsbet said.

Wren looked at her.

Elsbet heard how foolish it sounded. The child had almost been carried from her own cottage in a basket. Saying she did not have to go was mercy offered after the road had been taken.

"If Master Kest has men there," Elsbet said, "they will say I stirred you."

"He already says things."

"Saying can become paper."

"So can not seeing." Wren stepped over a half-drowned rail. "You cannot tell court what the meadow is if you think it is grass."

That stung. Elsbet knew meadow as ink: rent, dower, claim, unresolved. Ahead, where the land opened, Nine-Candle Meadow was neither flat nor green.

Water had taken it in pieces.

The higher ground still showed in long brown ribs, winter grass flattened and shining. Between them lay floodwater the color of stirred ale, running slow near the cottage and fast where it found the ferry cut. Fence stakes leaned like old teeth. A white goat stood on the cottage step with its beard dripping, offended by the end of the world. Beyond the meadow, the main bridge approach sagged into the river mist. Two carts waited on the far road, wool loads hooded under sacking, men shouting across the water as if volume could lay planks.

The ferry rope stretched from bank to bank, dark with wet, humming whenever the current pulled hard. On Wren's side, it ran through an iron ring bolted to a post as thick as Elsbet's waist. The post was notched and polished by years of hands. Below it, half under water, a plank landing knocked against its pegs.

Wren went still.

"What is wrong?" Elsbet asked.

"They moved the lower rope."

Elsbet followed her gaze. A second rope, newer and tar-black, had been tied from the old ferry post to a fresh stake driven farther up the bank, where the ground was still dry. A scrap of red cloth marked the knot. Not Lady Merrow's hawk. Not a court seal. Only manor color, cheap and certain.

"Who would move it?"

"Men who think my mother is finished using it."

Wren went down the slope before Elsbet could stop her. The mud sucked at the girl's boots. She reached the post and put both hands on the old wood, not embracing it, not quite. The current slapped the landing below her.

"Careful," Elsbet said.

"This ring was my father's," Wren said. "He paid the smith after lambing, and the smith cut three marks here so no one could call it borrowed." She pointed to cuts near the bolt, worn but visible beneath a skin of rain. "Mother said a paid thing needs scars."

Elsbet crouched beside her. The marks were not letters. They would not stand in court like a clean line, but they were there, older than Ruan's no husband entered.

"Do not touch the new rope," Elsbet said.

Wren's hand stopped. "It is on our post."

"That is why touching it helps him. He can say you damaged what court had to inspect."

"Court has not given it."

"No."

"Then why is it tied?"

Across the water a man shouted that he would pay double if someone brought the ferry over before the wool spoiled. Another voice shouted back that no crossing was lawful until the hearing settled the bank. The words broke in the rain, but their meaning reached well enough. Grain, wool, messages, sick people, lambs, debts, summons could pile up on one side of water and become money for whoever owned the rope.

Elsbet looked from the stranded carts to the new stake. She understood then what Ruan had understood before dawn. Nine-Candle Meadow was not a dead woman's pretty patch. It was a hand on the village throat whenever the bridge failed.

"He does not need it forever today," she said softly.

Wren turned. "What?"

"He needs it first. If his man holds the rope when court sits, everyone can call it order instead of theft."

"Then cut it."

"No."

"You always say no before I finish saving myself."

"Because saving yourself in a punishable way lets them choose the charge." Elsbet forced herself to stand. Her skirt was soaked to the thigh. "We need witnesses to see the rope was moved before the order, not a cut rope they can blame on you."

Wren stared at the red cloth as if hatred might set it smoking.

The cottage door banged in the wind.

Both of them turned.

The small house sat above the highest water on a stone pad, one room and a loft, smoke hole capped against rain. A basket stood by the door. Empty. Waiting. Elsbet knew it before Wren made a sound.

Wren ran.

"Wren!"

The girl splashed through the shallow runnel and up the cottage bank. Elsbet followed, slower, breath catching under her ribs. At the threshold, Wren kicked the basket hard enough to send it rolling into the wet grass. It tipped, filled at once, and began to sink as if the meadow wanted it.

"Good," Wren said, voice shaking. "Let it learn water."

Inside the cottage, nothing moved. That was almost worse. A room could be robbed loudly and still feel alive. This room had been counted. A stool by the hearth. Two bowls. A peg stripped bare except for a child's second shawl. The chest lid stood open.

Wren crossed to it.

Elsbet stayed at the door. "Should I come in?"

"You already did, in every way that matters."

It was not forgiveness. It was permission with teeth.

The chest held folded linen, a cracked cup wrapped in cloth, a man's spare shirt gone yellow at the seams, and a small wooden tally board. Wren lifted the board and looked beneath it, then exhaled.

"What?"

"She did not find the cord place."

"Good."

"She found almost everything else." Wren held up the tally board. It was marked with little cuts and burns. "Mother kept crossings here when people paid later. Father said paper drowned. Wood only lies if fire teaches it."

Elsbet did not take it. "Is it current?"

"Some. Not all. I was learning." Wren's fingers tightened. "If I bring it, they will say I made marks after. If I leave it, she comes back and burns it."

There was no answer that did not bite. Elsbet looked past Wren to the cold hearth and a candle stub guttered into a saucer. Human lives made records because human lives could be denied; then records needed protecting like children, and children like records, and there were never enough hands.

"Wrap it," Elsbet said. "You are not stealing from your own chest. If anyone asks, I told you not to show it unless court asks about unpaid crossings. It is not marriage proof. It proves this ferry was living."

Wren wrapped the board in the second shawl.

A horse snorted outside.

Not close at first. Down by the old post. Then another horse, and men's voices low enough to be dangerous.

Elsbet went to the window gap. Two manor men came along the higher bank, cloaks dark with rain: the broad-shouldered man from the chapel porch, and another with a mallet and three sharpened stakes. Behind them, a pony dragged a rope coil through the mud.

The broad man saw the kicked basket first. Then he looked up and saw Elsbet at the cottage window.

His smile was not large. It did not need to be.

"Mistress Vale," he called. "The chapel has grown legs."

Wren moved behind Elsbet with the wrapped tally board clutched flat against her chest.

Elsbet stepped out before the men could decide the doorway was theirs. Rain ran down her face and into her collar. She hoped it made her look calmer than she was.

"I came with Wren Dain to her mother's cottage," she said.

"Dain, is it?" The broad man set one hand on the old ferry post. "Court has not said so."

"Court has not said otherwise."

The man with the mallet laughed under his breath.

The broad one looked at the new red-marked rope, then at Elsbet's sleeves. She kept her hands open and away from the slat hidden inside.

"Master Kest said any stirring at the disputed bank should be remembered," he said.

"Then remember accurately. The child came to her own door. I witnessed men tying manor color before judgment."

His face changed a little at that. Not fear. Calculation finding a stone in its shoe.

"Flood work," he said. "Rope must be ready if the steward grants use."

"If," Elsbet said.

Wren's breath hitched behind her. Pride or terror. Maybe both.

Across the river, the stranded wool men shouted again. One lifted both arms, pleading or cursing. The current took his words. On the far rise beyond the broken bridge, a pale line of old stone showed through rain, dry-looking in the flood. No cart went that way. Roads bent around fear and charged the poor for the turning.

The broad manor man followed Elsbet's glance and spat into the mud.

"White-road weather," he said. "Makes fools of maps. Makes coin for ferries."

He picked up the sunken basket with two fingers and tossed it back toward the cottage step.

"Noon court," he said. "Bring the girl if the priest still wants to pretend she is chapel business. Bring yourself too, copyist. Master Kest will want to know what you came hunting."

Elsbet felt the slat in her sleeve, the bond strip beside it, and now Wren's wrapped tally board hidden behind her body. Not enough. Too much. Both.

"Tell Master Kest," she said, "that I came to see what his clean paper was standing in."

The man's eyes narrowed. For a moment she thought he might strike her. Instead he smiled again, smaller.

"Mud," he said. "Same as all of us."

The mallet fell behind him, driving a fresh stake into Wren's bank.

Elsbet did not move until the first blow ended. If she flinched, Wren would. If Wren ran, they would have their story.

Wood took iron. The meadow shuddered with each strike.

By the time the court bell began to ring noon from the village, Elsbet had seen enough to be summoned and not enough to save anyone.

That, she thought, was how men like Ruan built traps: not with locked doors, but with open ground no poor person could cross without leaving footprints.
