# Chapter 11 — Ward of Saint Brenna

Harl Brey's order did not sound like rescue when the clerk read it back.

It sounded like a fence made of wet sticks: chapel keeping, old toll tally, court pouch, no final wardship, no new lease. Each phrase held only because the next leaned against it. Each one made Wren safer by making her smaller.

The clerk stumbled over chapel keeping.

"Ward," Harl corrected.

The word moved through the room faster than candle smoke. Wren's hands closed around the marriage cord, then opened again, as if she had remembered that strangling proof would not help it breathe.

"Not wardship to the manor," Harl said, annoyed by how people heard what they feared. "Ward to Saint Brenna's custody until the disputed name is clean enough to enter or strike. Father Orlan answers for the child. Mistress Vale answers for the fair copy. Mistress Voss answers for her slat. Master Kest answers for the road accounts he has so helpfully endangered. Has everyone enough rope to hang themselves by form, or shall I issue more?"

No one laughed.

Ruan bowed as if insult could be folded and carried for later. "The ferry, Master Brey."

"Runs if the water demands it. Old tolls, not new. Coin to my clerk's pouch until I say otherwise. Anyone moving stakes or changing rope marks before the order is posted pays treble."

The wool men began talking at once. A farmer near the door asked who would pull rope if no one owned the crossing for the day. Harl pointed his pen at the broad manor man.

"You. Find two men who know the ferry and fear fines more than weather. They sign the pouch tally. If one penny vanishes, Master Kest may explain to Lady Merrow why flood income learned to swim."

The broad man looked to Ruan before he could stop himself.

Harl saw. So did half the room.

"Do not make me repeat the direction through your bailiff," Harl said.

The broad man reddened and went.

Elsbet stood with one hand near the rough register, though Father Orlan had already closed it. Her fingers smelled of old leather and smoke. All day she had imagined truth becoming heavier than fear. It had not. Fear only changed hands: into the court pouch, into Harl's roll, into Matra's shut mouth, into the way villagers looked at Wren and saw delayed ferry work before they saw a child.

Wren leaned close. "Am I theirs now?"

"No," Elsbet said.

The answer came too fast.

Wren heard the crack. "Am I yours?"

Father Orlan held his key cord with one hand and the wrapped register with the other. He seemed to have aged into both objects.

"You are under Saint Brenna's roof," he said.

"Roofs leak."

"This one especially." His mouth tried for a smile and failed before it became an insult. "But it is the roof the order names. I will answer for you."

"You already did," she said. It was not forgiveness. It was a receipt.

Matra Voss shut her candle box. The ninth court flame still burned on the table because no one had thought of putting it out, and perhaps because everyone feared what that would look like. Ruan watched the flame, then the box.

"Mistress Voss," he said.

"Master Kest."

"Your contract accounts will need cleaning after this disorder. Court lights arrived late."

"Court lights were lit."

"Late."

She lifted the box against her hip. "Then write late. Do not write false. They are different cuts."

Ruan's smile sharpened. "So many lessons today."

"No," Matra said. "Only people you taught badly."

For a moment Elsbet thought he might strike her with words that had legal edges. Instead he turned away from trade dirt, and his eyes found Elsbet.

"Your brother works hard."

The room seemed to tilt.

Father Orlan whispered, "Master Kest."

"A compliment, Father. I am told the miller values him. It would be a pity if household confusion made the boy's terms less forgiving. Young shoulders learn discipline quickly when kin bring disorder into court."

Wren looked at Elsbet, and the anger drained from her face, leaving something worse: understanding.

Elsbet felt Tomas's bond strip in her sleeve, softened by rain and handling. She had carried it all day like a small animal she might yet keep alive. "If you call his bond, call it for arrears. Do not dress spite as account."

"Accounts are made of conduct," Ruan said. "You taught the room that records should include what they mean."

Harl's pen stopped above the roll. "Master Kest. If the mill bond changes before this court closes its copy, I will wonder why every document you touch becomes lively only when crossed."

Ruan bowed lower than before. "Of course. No document will change before proper review."

Before, Elsbet thought. He was already choosing the hour after.

The court broke badly, as courts did: not with an ending, but with people remembering their own matters had become wet and late while someone else's truth took up the bench. Men pushed toward the yard. The grain woman passed Wren without speaking, then turned back and gave a quick, terrified nod. Wren nodded as if accepting a coin too small to spend.

At the doorway, the broad manor man returned with a leather pouch and two reluctant villagers behind him. Neither met Wren's eye. One held a ferry pole. The other had rope burns old enough to prove knowledge and fresh enough to prove need.

"Old tolls," Elsbet said before she could stop herself.

The broad man glared. "Court order says so."

"Then say it where the people waiting can hear."

He looked over her head toward Ruan, remembered Harl, and swore under his breath. Then he pushed into the rain and shouted, "Old tolls! Coin to court pouch! No lease changed!"

A cheer did not rise. Hungry people did not cheer delay. But the shout changed the yard. Men who had been ready to curse Wren for holding the rope cursed the pouch instead, and that gave a little room around her body.

Elsbet took it for what it was worth.

The walk back to Saint Brenna's chapel was shorter than the walk to court and heavier in every step. Father Orlan carried the register. Matra had gone toward her shed under a court boy's watch to fetch the original slat by evening bell. Wren walked between Elsbet and the priest, not because the order placed her there, but because both adults had learned enough to leave room for her to choose the middle.

Rain softened to mist. At the mill turning, Tomas waited under the eave with flour on his sleeves and a split lip.

Elsbet stopped so quickly Orlan almost struck her with the register bundle.

"Who did that?"

Tomas touched his mouth, then looked embarrassed by the blood as if it were another debt he should have hidden. "Sack hook slipped."

"Tomas."

His eyes flicked to Wren, then Orlan, then away. "Miller says I sleep at the mill tonight. Flood grinding. Extra hands. Bond terms allow it."

"Since when?"

"Since the court got noisy." He tried to smile. It pulled the split open again. "Do not make your face like that, Els. Better a night by the stones than breach written neat in the bond book."

Ruan had not changed the document before court closed. He had only moved the weight until it found Tomas's mouth.

Elsbet stepped toward her brother. He stepped back.

That hurt more than the lip.

"If you touch me," he said softly, "I will want to come with you. Then Miller says breach. Then Master Kest smiles with all his teeth."

Wren's fingers found the cord at her wrist. "I am sorry."

Tomas looked at her, properly looked, perhaps for the first time since she had come through the chapel door. "Do not be. My sister makes her own trouble."

"Tomas," Elsbet said.

"Good trouble," he added, and this time the smile stayed a little. "Mostly."

A bell rang from the manor yard, calling men to the ferry. Tomas flinched toward the sound because debt had trained his body before thought could argue.

Elsbet wanted to promise him something: by sunset, by tomorrow, by Saint Brenna's hard little mercy. Promises were cheap when someone else paid the coin.

"I will read the bond before they mark anything new," she said.

"You cannot read if they do not show you."

"Then I will make them show me where people can see."

Tomas nodded as if that were enough because he loved her and was young enough to help her lie kindly. Then he turned back under the mill eave.

At the chapel, Father Orlan unlocked the door with hands that shook only after the key found the hole. Inside, the room still held the morning's damp and the blank place where the fair copy waited. Wren stood on the threshold with the look she had worn at her cottage: measuring what had been taken, what remained, and what taking might still be called care.

"Where do wards sleep?" she asked.

Father Orlan looked toward the sacristy, then the bench by the brazier, then at the chest as if the register might advise him.

"Not by the chest," Wren said. "I am not another locked thing."

"No," he said. "By the brazier, then. With the door bar where you can see it."

Elsbet fetched the spare funeral pall because it was the thickest cloth the chapel owned and because calling it bedding would have made everyone pretend. Wren took one corner and helped spread it on the flags. The cord stayed on her wrist. Mud dried on her hem. She looked very small and not at all defeated.

Father Orlan set the wrapped register back into the chest. He added the copied slat and the household tally board, then stopped before closing the lid.

"Wren," he said.

She came close enough to see, not close enough to be invited into trust too easily.

"Under court order," he said, "I keep these until they are called. Under your eyes, I lock them. Under Saint Brenna's roof, I answer if they vanish."

"Under my mother's name," Wren said, "you open it again."

He bowed his head. "Under her name."

The key turned.

Elsbet went to the copying board. The fair copy lay where she had left it, still unfinished, still refusing to be harmless. Beside it, Harl's clerk had sent a narrow order strip with wet sand clinging to the ink.

Copyist Vale to prepare margin cause in the fair copy hand. No seal before review. Name disputed in open court.

Her name. Not just her hand now. Her name on an order. Her safe invisibility had been entered and lost.

Wren came to stand beside her. "What will you write?"

Elsbet looked at Maura's line, at the blank margin waiting to become either shelter or trap, at the rain-dark door beyond which Tomas walked back into debt.

"Only what can stand," she said.

The words were Ruan's, once.

In Elsbet's mouth they tasted like iron.
