# Chapter 9 — Before the Flood Court

Harl Brey did not sit.

That was the first thing Elsbet noticed. Men who meant to listen sat down so others could arrange themselves around patience. Men who meant to end confusion stayed standing.

The steward remained at the edge of the dais with one hand on the back of his chair and the other held out for the court clerk's roll. He was narrow in every part: narrow shoulders under the dry brown robe, narrow mouth, narrow eyes that moved from object to object as if people were badly stored papers. Rain had not touched him. That seemed less like comfort than accusation.

"Master Kest," he said again, "answer plainly. Why has my court gone quiet?"

Ruan bowed the depth due a man whose authority he needed and not a finger's width more. "A copyist has mistaken private anxiety for court business. I was attempting to spare your time before the wardship matter."

"My time was not spared. It was interrupted." Harl's eyes shifted to Elsbet. "Name."

Her mouth had gone dry. The room watched in pieces: turned shoulders, tilted caps, the goose basket gone still, Wren holding the cord open between both hands. Father Orlan stood by the register table with his wrapped book, looking like a man carrying his own sentence.

"Elsbet Vale," she said. "Copyist for Saint Brenna's chapel."

"Are you confessing failure to produce the quarterly fair copy?"

The question was a hook with no wool on it. Confessing failure made the delay hers. Denying failure made Ruan's private draft look like the only clean writing in the room.

"I am giving cause for not sealing it."

A little movement crossed the benches. Cause was a court word. Elsbet had not meant to sound brave. She had meant to sound exact.

Harl looked at his clerk. The clerk dipped his pen and wrote three small words. "Cause stated," Harl said. "What cause?"

Ruan's hand rested beside his draft on the side table. He did not touch it. He did not need to. The words on it sat clean and patient: Maura Reed, called Dain in local use.

Elsbet lifted the unsealed fair copy. "The manor wording asks Saint Brenna's copy to enter Maura as unmarried for the wardship and ferry claim. The rough chapel register records Maura Reed joined to Edric Dain before Saint Brenna's door. The death entry names her Maura Dain and marks nine burial candles. I cannot make those lines into no husband entered without making a false clean copy."

Harl's face did not soften. Good, Elsbet thought wildly. Softness could be pulled apart.

"You accuse the manor copy?"

"I accuse no chest I have not seen."

Ruan smiled at that, quick and thin.

Elsbet made herself continue. "I say the chapel copy is not fair if it omits a recorded marriage and a disputed candle count."

Harl took two steps down from the dais. People leaned back as if his robe might stain them with consequence. "Father Orlan Rusk. Is the rough register present?"

Father Orlan bowed his head. "Under my key, Master Brey."

"That was not my question."

"Yes. It is present."

"Is it altered?"

The old priest's face tightened. "No."

"Since when?"

For a moment Orlan looked toward Elsbet, and she hated him for wanting rescue even now. Then Wren said, not loudly, "Since before my mother died."

The broad manor man stepped forward. "Girl, you are not called."

Wren's fingers closed over the cord.

Harl looked at her as one might look at a loose page blown under a door. "And you are?"

"Wren Dain."

Ruan said, "Wren Reed, called Dain in the summons."

The cord twitched in Wren's hands. Elsbet felt the child's correction rise like flame and prayed it would not burn too soon.

Harl held up one finger. "The court will call names. It does not receive them by shouting. If the child's name is the dispute, every person in this room will stop helping me dirty the roll before the entry is reached."

He did not sound kind. He sounded offended by mess.

The clerk wrote again.

Harl turned back to Orlan. "Has the register been out of chapel custody?"

"No."

"Who has handled it?"

"Myself. Elsbet Vale during appointed copying. No other."

"Master Kest?"

Ruan's smile did not falter. "I have never touched Saint Brenna's rough book. I have asked only for the fair copy due to this court."

"Then we have, at minimum, an unsealed chapel copy, a rough book under priestly custody, and a bailiff's prepared wording which the copyist says cannot be matched." Harl's eyes went to the cord in Wren's hands. "What is that?"

Wren looked at Elsbet, not for permission. For warning.

"A marriage cord," Elsbet said before Ruan could name it ribbon. "Brought by Wren from her mother's keeping. It supports the register line. It does not stand alone."

"Generous of it," Harl said.

Some men on the benches laughed because they thought the steward had invited them to. He did not look at them, but the laugh died anyway.

"Any other material proof dragged into my court without order?" he asked.

There. The open door and the locked one together.

Elsbet said, "A copied candle slat and a household ferry tally are under chapel key. The candlewright's original slat remains with Matra Voss. A route mark at the Glass Mile bend was seen covered by manor cloth on the way here, but it is not marriage proof."

Ruan made a soft sound. "Roadside mud, trade kindling, a child's string, and a priest's failing eyes. Master Brey, the bridge approach sinks while we sort village toys."

"The bridge approach sinks whether you speak or not." Harl took the clerk's roll and scanned the first page. "Where is Matra Voss?"

"At her shed," Ruan said. "Fulfilling a candle order for this court, unless Mistress Vale has persuaded her to neglect it."

Elsbet's stomach tightened. Matra had refused to come unless called. That refusal had been survival, not cowardice. Survival looked poor in rooms built for summons.

"Was she summoned for this matter?" Harl asked.

Ruan spread his hands. "The matter was not supposed to be a matter. The child had no clean marriage entry before the manor. The ferry claim is urgent."

"You moved the court to meet urgency. You did not summon the candlewright whose marks might complicate urgency. Efficient. Not necessarily clean."

Ruan's face changed only around the eyes.

Harl handed the roll back to the clerk. "I dislike surprises. I dislike them more when they arrive wearing my court's seal."

For one foolish heartbeat Elsbet hoped.

Then Harl looked at her and killed the hope before it could become dependence. "This does not mean your objection has merit. It means I will not have a wardship order appealed because a bailiff and a copyist chose a corner argument over form."

"There is no appeal before hunger," Wren said.

This time no one laughed.

Harl's gaze settled on her. "If you speak again uncalled, child, I will have you stand outside until your name becomes convenient to others. Do you understand?"

Wren went white with rage. She nodded once.

Elsbet wanted to strike him. She wanted to thank him. She did neither. Court made ugly rooms inside people.

Ruan stepped toward the dais. "Then call the wardship now. Let the chapel read what it claims to read. If there is cause, you may decide whether to send for the candlewright. If there is not, we settle the ferry before the road men tear up the benches for planks."

The wool men muttered. A farmer near the door said, "Ferry first," and then looked sorry he had been heard. Others took it up under their breath, not because they hated Wren, Elsbet thought, but because wet grain did not wait for justice to become legible.

Ruan had placed them all on his scale again.

Harl listened to the room count its own need. His expression gave nothing away. At last he sat, and the chair's legs scraped once against the boards.

"Very well," he said. "The court will hear the wardship and ferry claim before repairs. Not because Master Kest requests it. Because every later fee depends on whose rope may lawfully cross the water. Clerk, enter: disputed chapel cause before wardship. Father Orlan, bring the rough register to the table. Mistress Vale, stand where your hand can be seen. Child, hold whatever household object you have brought and do not mistake possession for proof."

Wren lifted the cord a little higher. Her hands shook. She made no sound.

Father Orlan unwrapped the register. The old book came into the smoky court light like something dug up alive. Its cracked cover, stained pages, and swollen spine looked indecent beside the clerk's clean roll. People craned despite themselves. They knew that book. Not with eyes, most of them. With fees paid, candles begged, vows made, children named, dead carried through rain.

Ruan returned to the manor table. His draft lay there with Elsbet's court pen still crossed over it. He removed the pen, wiped the nib though she had not written, and set it neatly beside the sheet.

Then he turned to the clerk. "Matter of Wren Reed, called Dain," he said.

The clerk hesitated only long enough to see whether Harl would correct the wording.

Harl did not.

The pen scratched.

Wren's cord stretched between her fists until Elsbet thought the old knots would break.

The clerk raised his voice for the room.

"Wardship of Wren Reed, called Dain; cottage, meadow, ferry bank, and household claim of the late Maura Reed, called Dain. Chapel cause entered against the fair copy. Let the candles be counted if Saint Brenna's people insist they can count."

Elsbet stepped to the table beside the rough register. The page edges were soft with years of frightened hands.

"We can count," she said, and hoped counting would be enough to make the room hear what numbers could not carry.
