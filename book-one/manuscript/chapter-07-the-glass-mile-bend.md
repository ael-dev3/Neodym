# Chapter 7 — The Glass Mile Bend

Rain made a procession of them.

The broad manor man led with the summons bell, striking it whenever a door opened too slowly or a dog barked. Father Orlan followed with the rough register wrapped under his cloak like a sick child. Wren kept beside him, refusing to walk behind her mother's name. Elsbet came last, one hand on the damp summons, the other close enough to catch Wren if mud took her.

Court bells left no room for warning Tomas or calling Matra. They made every decent errand look like disobedience.

The lane below the chapel had become a brown runnel carrying straw, ash, and one drowned mouse. Villagers stood in doorways with shawls over their heads. Some crossed themselves for the register. Some looked away from Wren, performing the public art of not seeing what might require speech.

At the mill turning, the road split.

The lower way ran toward the manor yard and flood court. Yesterday mud; now water shoulder-deep to the hedges, stake tops showing like teeth. A cart stood axle-sunk where its driver had tried the crossing before sense caught him. Two men were unloading sacks onto a higher bank and cursing softly enough to count as prayer.

The upper way bent east along the ridge.

No one named it at first.

A pale line of stone showed beyond the wet thorn, almost level with the road and too dry-looking in the rain. It did not shine. It held itself apart from the weather, white under gray waterlight, each flat stone set close until mist swallowed the line. The Glass Mile. From the ferry meadow it had been a seam on the far rise. Close by, it looked less like wonder than an old command.

The broad man stopped striking the bell.

Father Orlan's breath caught, or the register slipped under his arm. Wren looked from the white stones to the drowned lower road.

"Court is that way," she said, nodding at the flood.

"Court is where the steward sits," the broad man said. "The steward sits where Master Kest gets him dry." He pointed up the ridge with the bell handle. "We go by the bend."

"By that?" Orlan asked.

"Near it. Not on it. Unless you want to carry the book through belly water."

A woman with two grain sacks tied over a donkey's back had come up behind them. Her donkey saw the pale road, planted all four feet, and began to tremble. The woman slapped its neck, then stopped when the animal did not even flick an ear.

"No banners on white stone," she muttered.

"No one has a banner," the broad man snapped.

He looked down at Lady Merrow's red hawk stitched on his wet cloak, folded the corner inward, and held it there.

Elsbet saw Wren see it. That was how the Glass Mile worked on Harrowick Ford: not by thunder or any hand from old stone, but by making strong men hide cloth from rain.

They took the upper way.

The ridge path narrowed after the first rise. Floodwater lapped below on both sides, turning gardens and ditches into one skin. The white stones ran parallel beyond winter grass, unmarked by hoof or wheel. Where the ridge path sagged close, someone had planted hazel wands and tied undyed thread between them, a poor fence against fear.

"Do not stare," Father Orlan said.

Wren stared harder.

"Does it take eyes?" she asked.

"It takes foolishness and gives back trouble."

"That is what court does."

The broad man barked a laugh before remembering who he served. Orlan did not rebuke her. He shifted the register higher under his cloak and kept walking.

At the bend, the ridge widened around an old way-post.

It was black oak, cracked at the top, an iron ring rusted into one side, three crossbars pointing to places the flood had made uncertain. MANOR YARD softened on the lower bar. FERRY BANK was cut newer and rougher on the middle. The highest bar pointed toward the white road and bore no words.

A strip of red cloth had been tied around the middle bar.

Fresh. Manor red. Wet dye had run down the old oak like a wound.

Wren stopped so suddenly that Elsbet nearly struck her.

"That is ours," Wren said.

The broad man turned. "The post belongs to the road."

"The mark under it." She moved closer before Elsbet could catch her sleeve. "There. Under your cloth."

"Do not touch it," Elsbet said.

Wren's hand froze above the knot.

The grain woman had come up with her trembling donkey. Behind her, two wool men and a boy with hens waited because there was nowhere else to wait.

Elsbet stepped beside Wren and looked without touching.

Under the red cloth, partly hidden, the middle bar carried little cuts. Not letters. Lease scars, perhaps, or work marks: three old vertical strokes near the iron ring, a fourth newer and slantwise, filled with dark tar, and a half-moon rubbed with ash so it could be seen in rain.

"Mother cut that at Candlemas," Wren said. "When the thaw rope went up. She said the road must know the ferry was awake."

The broad man spat over the ridge. "Children make customs out of scratches."

"Then why cover it?" Elsbet asked.

He looked at her as if she had tugged a rope tied to his teeth.

"Road warning," he said. "Ferry under court holding."

"Court has not held it yet."

"Court bell rang."

"Bell is not judgment."

The wool men muttered at that. One said, "If judgment would cross the water faster, I would pay it." The other elbowed him quiet.

Father Orlan had come near enough to see the post, though he squinted badly. Rain gathered on his brows and ran into the lines beside his nose.

"Those are ferry wake marks," he said.

The broad man's face tightened. "Priest sees well when it suits him."

"No," Orlan said. "Poorly. That is why old marks are cut deep. Ferrymen paid a post nail at chapel when flood service opened: chapel took the nail fee, manor the road fee, and everyone pretended the split was holy because it kept arguments small."

Elsbet stared at him. "Is it in the register?"

"Some years. Not all." He looked ashamed before she could ask why. "Small fees were sometimes written in candle margins. Sometimes only the nail was counted."

Not enough, then. Again not enough. But not nothing.

Wren leaned toward the bar. "That tar is this year. I watched Mother heat it. She burned her thumb and said saints liked to be paid in skin because coin bored them."

A hen clucked sharply inside the boy's crate. The donkey gave a low, miserable sound and pulled against its rope, away from the white stones.

The broad man reached for the red strip.

"Leave it," Elsbet said.

"You told the girl not to touch it."

"I told her not to alter what court may need to see. You have already covered it. If you uncover it now, people should remember whose hand moved cloth."

The grain woman made a small sound, almost approval and almost fear.

The broad man let the cloth go. Red dye dripped from its lower edge onto the road mud.

"You think a wet ribbon saves her meadow?" he asked.

"No," Elsbet said. "I think it shows someone knew what had to be hidden."

He stepped close enough that she smelled horse sweat in his cloak. "Careful, copyist. You are summoned to answer, not to collect roadside stories."

"Then take us to the room where answers count."

For a moment she thought he would strike her after all. But too many people stood there with too much need for the ferry, and the red strip hung behind him, covering a mark everyone now wanted either to use or forget.

He rang the handbell once, violently.

The donkey bolted. Its rope tore free; it lurched toward the white stones, stopped short, and split its sacks. Barley spilled across wet grass beside the old road. For a heartbeat no one moved. Every hand had learned caution before kindness.

Wren went first. She did not step on the white stones. She crouched beside them and pulled the torn sack upright. Elsbet followed; then the boy with hens, one wool man, and Father Orlan, who knelt with the register held high under his cloak and swept grain from the pale edge.

The broad man did not help.

He did not stop them either.

When the sack was tied badly enough to hold, the grain woman gripped Wren's wrist. "Your mother's ferry took my sister over in fever week," she said low. "No charge till thaw. I remember the blue cord on her door."

Wren's face changed so quickly it hurt to see. Hope could be as cruel as fear when it arrived without warning.

"Will you say it?" Wren asked.

The woman looked toward the court road, then the red cloth, then her donkey. "If called."

If called. Harrowick Ford's bravest measure.

Elsbet did not press. A frightened witness broken on the ridge would be no witness at all. "Stand in the court yard if you can," she said. "Let the steward see you came by the bend."

The woman nodded once.

The broad man rang the bell again, less violently this time. "Enough charity. Court waits."

They left the way-post with the red cloth still tied around the ferry bar. They left the white stones behind. The ridge path turned west and descended toward the manor yard, where smoke rose from wet roofs and the court house showed its open doors like a mouth already deciding what it would swallow.

Ruan Kest stood under the eave.

He saw Father Orlan's covered register first. Then Wren's cord around her wrist. Then Elsbet's face. Last of all, his eyes went past them to the broad man, and to the red dye staining the man's fingers.

For one breath Ruan looked angry. Then he smiled, dry as paper kept from rain.

"Mistress Vale," he said. "The court began without your roadside sermon."

Elsbet felt barley grit under one fingernail and the summons damp against her wrist. Behind her, the road bent around fear. Ahead, fear had benches, seals, and a clerk's clean hand.

"No," she said. "It began on the road."
