# Chapter 1 — The Fair Copy

The ink would not dry.

Elsbet Vale held the page near the chapel brazier until the damp began to curl it, then drew it back with a curse small enough for Saint Brenna to forgive. Outside, rain worried at the shutters. It had not yet become a proper spring downpour, only a thin cold tapping, but the river heard such things before people did. By noon the ford would be brown to its knees. By dusk the lower road might be gone.

By dusk, Father Orlan had said, the fair copy must be sealed.

Elsbet set the page flat and pressed one clean corner. The sheet was good rag paper, too good for mistakes, bought with chapel coin. Births down the left. Marriages below. Deaths and burial candles on the second page. Tithe promises after that, if there was space and if the poor could promise anything without lying to God.

The rough register lay open beside it like an old animal, spine split, pages greasy from years of thumbs. It smelled of wax smoke, damp wool, and the sour barley beer Father Orlan pretended he did not drink before winter burials. Elsbet knew its stains better than some faces in Harrowick Ford: soot near Saint’s Day, a baby’s ash-black footmark, old grief pressed flat between fees.

Her fair copy had none of that. That was the trouble with fair copies. They looked innocent.

A bell knocked once in the yard. Not the chapel bell in the little tower, but the iron handbell at the gate, hard-struck by someone who did not care whether a saint was sleeping.

Father Orlan flinched at the sound. He sat by the sacristy chest with his key cord twisted around two fingers, lips moving over entries he could no longer see without bringing his nose almost to the page. His eyes had gone milk-blue at the rims. In candlelight they looked holy. In daylight they looked tired.

“Do not hurry the M’s,” he said.

“Then tell men not to strike bells like debt collectors.”

Father Orlan’s mouth twitched, but the bell came again, and the twitch died. He pushed himself upright. His knees made a sound like two sticks stepped on in mud.

“Keep the register open only to this quarter.”

“It is open only to this quarter.”

“And if Master Kest asks—”

“I do not hand him the rough book.”

Father Orlan looked at her, not blindly. “You do not hand anyone the rough book.”

Elsbet dipped her pen because her fingers wanted something useful to do. “I know the rule. I have heard it every quarter since I was sixteen.”

“Rules are easier to keep before the bailiff is in the room.”

The gate bell struck a third time.

“Then let him stand in the rain and improve himself,” Elsbet said, but she covered the rough register with a square of linen before Father Orlan reached the door.

Ruan Kest entered as if the chapel had been built around his errand. Rain shone on his black riding coat; a leather tube sat under one arm; two manor men crowded the porch and dripped on the threshold stones. He was not tall. He had taught Harrowick Ford to remember him as one by making everyone else step aside.

“Father,” he said.

“Master Kest.” Father Orlan did not offer warmth. He did not offer refusal either.

Ruan’s eyes moved to the copying board, the covered register, the brazier, Elsbet’s ink-stained hand. “Mistress Vale. Good. A steady hand saves the court from many quarrels.”

“A steady hand causes some,” Elsbet said.

Father Orlan made a warning noise in his throat.

Ruan smiled as if she had passed him a cup. “Not when it writes what is already settled.” He laid the leather tube on the table. Its end was capped in red wax stamped with Lady Merrow’s small hawk. Not the lady’s own hand, of course. Nothing so high came to Harrowick Ford unless it wanted rent. “Flood court is moved to tomorrow’s first bell.”

Elsbet’s pen stopped above the page.

Father Orlan said, “It was set for third day.”

“The bridge approach has sunk another foot. Two wool carts are stuck at Moat Lane, and a grain team came back with one horse lame.” Ruan brushed rain from his cuff. “Unless you prefer the court sit after half the witnesses are stranded and the road money has washed away.”

Road money. Not sheep lame in flood ruts, not women hauling sacks the long way, not boys paid a farthing to stand in water and hold ferry rope until their hands went blue. Road money.

Father Orlan rubbed his key cord. “The fair copy is not finished.”

“That is why I came early.” Ruan opened the tube and slid out a folded sheet. “The manor has prepared the disputed entries for matching. Births, deaths, ward claims, ferry leases. You need only keep your chapel copy clean against it.”

Elsbet looked at the sheet but did not touch it.

Ruan noticed. “You may read, Mistress Vale. No one has accused your eyes of failing.”

Father Orlan’s face tightened.

Elsbet took the sheet by the edges. Manor paper was thicker than chapel paper and smelled faintly of cedar chest. The hand was not Ruan’s. Too round, too careful, a clerk’s hand trained to make lies look bored. She read past two births, a fever death, three tithe arrears, and came to the line that mattered because Ruan had placed his thumb near it without seeming to.

Maura Dain, deceased winter fever, one child Wren, no husband entered, cottage and meadow claim unresolved pending wardship.

No husband entered.

Outside, a cart wheel hit a rut in the lane with a wet crack. Someone shouted after the horse. In the chapel, the brazier gave a soft collapse of ash.

Elsbet kept her face still. A copyist’s face was part of her wage. People brought grief, pride, shame, and bargains to the chapel table, then watched the mouth above the hand. A twitch could cost a fee. A wince could start a rumor.

“This says no husband entered,” she said.

“So does the manor chest.” Ruan’s voice stayed mild.

“I have not seen the manor chest.”

“No. You have seen the chapel book. That is why we require agreement.”

Father Orlan reached for the sheet. Ruan did not release it until the priest’s fingers trembled in the air between them. Then he let go.

Elsbet lifted the linen from the rough register.

“Careful,” Father Orlan whispered.

She turned back three pages, not because she needed to hunt but because it was safer to appear to hunt. Maura’s name had sat in her mind since Candlemas. Poor women’s names often did. They came attached to unpaid fees, late baptisms, burial candles bought one at a time. Maura had paid in clipped copper and ferry tokens, with Wren beside her like a thin brown sparrow in a shawl too large.

There. Marriage entries, late autumn before the first ice.

Maura Reed joined by cord and witness to Edric Dain, ferryman’s son, before Saint Brenna’s door. Fee short by two pence, promised after lambing. Witnesses: Orlan Rusk, Matra Voss, Jory Pell by mark.

Elsbet’s own younger hand had squeezed the last witness into the margin after Jory made his mark backwards. She remembered the cord, blue and yellow, not new but clean. She remembered Edric Dain smelling of river cold. Dead now. Men who crossed for winter work did not always cross back.

“Father,” she said.

“I know.” His voice was so low she almost missed it.

Ruan heard enough. “You know what?”

Father Orlan laid one hand over his eyes. “The entry is unclear.”

Elsbet looked up.

The old priest did not look back.

Ruan’s smile returned, smaller. “Age makes many things unclear. Ink fades. Margins crowd. Poor witnesses put marks where they should not. That is why courts prefer clean copies.”

“The entry is not faded,” Elsbet said.

“Then perhaps it is mistaken.”

“By my hand?”

“If you like. You were young.”

“I was eighteen, not drunk.”

One of the manor men in the porch snorted. Ruan did not turn. “No one is asking you to confess to drunkenness. Only to avoid carrying forward an uncertain line into a court matter with road consequences.”

“There is a child consequence.”

“There are always child consequences.” Ruan’s mildness thinned. “There are also bridge consequences, ferry consequences, grain consequences, wool consequences, and debt consequences. Harrowick Ford is not kept alive by old cord knots.”

The rain strengthened. It ticked faster on the shutters and darkened the stone sill near the eyeless clay Brenna, where people still left pins when babies would not sleep.

Father Orlan said, “Master Kest, give us until dusk.”

“You have until dusk. The seal must be dry before tomorrow’s first bell.” Ruan tapped the manor sheet. “Write the death as shown. Leave the husband line absent. The steward will decide wardship.”

“Wardship where?” Elsbet asked, though she knew better than to ask questions with answers shaped like traps.

Ruan folded his gloves together. “Mistress Dain’s child cannot hold a ferry meadow alone.”

“She has a name.”

“Children have many names before court. Fewer after.”

Father Orlan’s hand tightened on the table edge.

Ruan looked at Elsbet’s unfinished page. “You will be paid the full copy fee tonight. Your brother’s mill bond is also due for review, I think.”

Ink slid from Elsbet’s pen and made a black bead on the clean paper. Not on a word, thank the saint. In the blank between marriages and deaths.

“Tomas has paid two months,” she said.

“Tomas has paid late two months.” Ruan’s eyes were flat now. “A household that keeps its marks clean has friends in a flood season. A household that smears them may find the road expensive.”

There it was. Not a threat anyone could write down. Only weather. Only roads. Only the way powerful men made the world lean until poor people fell in the useful direction.

Father Orlan said, “Enough.”

Ruan gave him a little bow, proper this time and therefore insulting. “Dusk.” He slid the manor sheet back into its tube. “I will send for the copy before the court bell. Do not make me send twice.”

He left wet footprints down the aisle. His men followed. Men like Ruan did not announce departure. They left others to count what had changed.

For a while the chapel held only rain, brazier ash, and Father Orlan’s breathing.

Elsbet blotted the ink bead with sand. Her hands felt cold and clumsy. She wanted to say the clean, brave thing: enter Maura’s marriage as written, seal it, and let Ruan choke on his own neat omission. But Tomas’s face had risen in her mind at once, sixteen and flour-haired, trying to laugh when the miller cuffed him for dropping a sack. Debt made a body into something other people could schedule.

Father Orlan sank back onto his stool.

“You said unclear,” Elsbet said.

His shame was quiet. That made it harder to hate. “If the manor challenges the entry as crowded or mistaken, they can question every marriage I sealed these last five years.”

“It is not unclear.”

“No.”

“Then why say it?”

He looked toward the locked chest, toward the poor battered book that held half the village together by string and habit. “Because if the chapel loses its seal, Ruan will not need to ask next time.”

Elsbet looked down. The marriage line sat cramped but legible, Maura’s name joined to Dain in brown ink dried years before anyone thought to profit from doubting it.

Below, on the next page, in the death entries for winter fever, Maura appeared again. Not as Reed. Not as uncertain. Maura Dain, nine candles, one child, ferry bank.

Nine candles. Not eight, as the manor death summary had said.

Elsbet touched that line with the clean end of her pen and felt, absurdly, as if the book had a pulse.

“Father,” she said.

“No more today.”

“There is another mismatch.”

He closed his eyes.

The rain came harder, and somewhere beyond the chapel wall the river bell began to ring for rising water.
