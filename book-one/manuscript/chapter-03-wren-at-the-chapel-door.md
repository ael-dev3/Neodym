# Chapter 3 — Wren at the Chapel Door

No one answered Wren Dain at first.

The rain did it for them, spilling from the chapel roof in cold ropes and pattering off the threshold stones. Wren stood inside the open door as if she had been placed there by the weather and dared them to move her. Her shawl clung to her narrow shoulders. Mud striped her skirt to the knees. Both hands stayed hidden beneath the wool at her chest.

Tomas took one step back from the table.

Wren saw it. “You too?”

He flushed. “I did not do anything.”

“That is what grown folk say before they do it.”

“I am sixteen.”

“Then you are old enough to know if a name is being stolen.” Her eyes came back to Elsbet. They were not the soft eyes grief was supposed to give a child. They were hot and dry and too awake. “Are you writing it down or rubbing it out?”

Father Orlan shut the chapel door with care, as though quiet wood could make the question smaller. “Wren, you should not be out in this water.”

“I should not be in a cousin’s kitchen either.”

Elsbet felt the words strike the table before she understood them. “Who said that to you?”

“Master Kest’s woman.”

“His wife?” Tomas asked.

“His cousin’s wife. The one with the little mouth.” Wren’s fingers moved under the shawl. “She came to the cottage after noon. She counted the pots. She counted the blankets. She said if the court placed me proper, I could be useful by spring shearing.”

Father Orlan whispered something that might have been a prayer if prayers could sound ashamed.

Elsbet looked at the fair copy. The clean blank after Maura’s death line seemed to widen.

“Did she have a wardship order?” Elsbet asked.

Wren’s lip curled. “She had a basket.”

“A basket?”

“For my things.”

That was worse than a paper. A paper admitted a thing was not yet done. A basket was how powerful people taught the poor to feel the future arriving.

“Did she touch anything?” Father Orlan asked.

Wren looked at him as if he had disappointed her by needing to ask. “She touched everything like it had already forgotten me.”

The thing under her shawl shifted again.

Elsbet held out a hand, palm up, not reaching. “What did you bring?”

Wren did not move.

“I will not take it from you.”

“People say that with one hand.”

“Then look at both.” Elsbet set her pen down and showed her empty palms, ink-stained and cold. “I am asking because if Master Kest’s household was in your cottage, anything left there may not stay yours by morning.”

Wren stood there another long moment, measuring her. Then she pulled a kerchief from inside the shawl. It had once been white. Fever smoke, years of chest dust, and rain had made it the color of old bone. She unwrapped it on the table between the covered register and the unfinished fair copy.

The cord lay inside, blue and yellow, faded unevenly where fingers had held it. Two joining knots sat near the middle. One end had been singed dark, not burned through. The other carried a small bead of hardened wax.

Father Orlan made a wounded sound.

Wren heard it and turned on him. “You knew it.”

“I knew a cord was laid.”

“You tied it.”

“At the marriage, yes.”

“And now you remember only after they ask to make her not married?”

The old priest had no answer that would not make him smaller.

Elsbet wanted to speak for him. That frightened her more than Wren’s anger. She did not want the habit of protecting weak men from hard questions. She looked at the cord instead.

“May I touch it?”

Wren’s hand closed around one end. “Say first what it does.”

“It may help.”

“That is what people say when it does not.”

“It does not prove enough alone.” Elsbet made herself say it cleanly. “A cord can be kept, moved, borrowed, stolen, or called another cord. If I carry only this to court, Ruan will say a grieving child brought a household ribbon and we dressed it up as law.”

“It is not ribbon.”

“I know.”

“Then write that.”

“If I write more than the record can bear, he will break the whole record with it.” Elsbet tapped the rough register under the linen. “This says your mother was joined to Edric Dain. The death line says she was buried with the household light. The cord can stand beside those things. It cannot stand in their place.”

Wren stared at her. “Truth needs friends, then.”

“Yes.”

“That is a stupid way to keep truth.”

“Yes.”

Tomas gave a short laugh, not because anything was funny. It escaped him like breath from a split sack. Wren looked at him again.

“Will your bond be safe if mine is not?” she asked.

His face went white beneath the flour paste. “I do not want your meadow.”

“No. You only want her to be afraid enough.”

“Wren,” Father Orlan said.

“No.” She put both hands on the table, one still holding the cord. “Everyone says Master Kest’s name soft. Master Kest says court soft. The woman with the basket said placement soft. My mother said some words have wool on them so you do not hear the knife.”

Elsbet felt Tomas look at her. He had asked whether the truth was worth his bond. Wren had answered from the other side of the same blade.

“My brother is not your enemy,” Elsbet said.

“Then why does his danger count aloud and mine waits in the margin?”

The chapel went still except for rain and the river bell. Elsbet had no clean defense. She could say Tomas was her blood. She could say blood was not justice but it was weight. She could say Wren was twelve and cruel because fear had found no softer place to sit. None of that would dry the ink.

“I have counted his danger louder,” Elsbet said.

Wren blinked once. It was the first childlike thing she had done.

Father Orlan touched the back of the stool but did not sit. “Wren, if the cord is seen by the wrong hands before court, they may claim it was planted after the dispute.”

“It was in my mother’s chest.”

“Can anyone else swear to that?”

“My mother is dead.”

“I know.”

“Then stop asking dead people to do work the living are too scared for.”

The words should have been too large for her mouth. They were not. They fit because she had been made to grow around them.

Elsbet drew the work scrap back from under her hand. The line she had copied from the death page looked thin and lonely. Maura Dain. Nine lights. One child. Ferry bank. She wrote beneath it: cord brought by Wren, blue and yellow, two joining knots, singed end, wax bead.

Father Orlan leaned forward. “That is not the fair copy.”

“No.”

Wren watched the pen. “Why not?”

“Because if I put this on the fair copy now without witness, Ruan will call the whole sheet poisoned. But if Matra’s slats show the ninth candle paid under Maura Dain, and if you can say where this cord was kept, and if Father Orlan will say he tied such a cord, then the court cannot swallow the omission without chewing in front of everyone.”

Father Orlan closed his eyes at the word will.

“You will say it,” Wren said to him.

The priest opened his eyes.

“You stood at the door when they tied it. You watched my mother put her hand in his. You took the fee short and said after lambing would do. I remember because she laughed when you said sheep were holier than coin because at least sheep multiplied.”

Father Orlan’s face changed. Not much. Enough.

“I did say that,” he whispered.

“Then remember louder.”

A hard knock struck the side door.

Tomas jolted. Wren snatched the cord back into her fist. Father Orlan reached for the linen as if the register could be made invisible by hope.

The knock came again. “From Master Kest,” a boy’s voice called through the wood. “For the chapel copy.”

No one moved.

Elsbet folded the work scrap once and slid it beneath the rough register, not hidden from herself, not thrown away. Then she lifted the fair copy. The page was still clean where Ruan wanted absence. Clean enough to be dangerous.

“Father,” she said, “do not seal it.”

His key cord trembled against his belt. “Elsbet.”

“Do not seal it.”

“If I refuse without form—”

“Then take form.” She put the fair sheet back on the board and set her pen across it, not writing, not yet. “The chapel has found a disputed death candle tied to a disputed marriage entry. The copyist requires witness before making fair. That is form enough to last one night.”

The boy outside kicked mud from his shoes. “Father?”

Father Orlan looked older than he had at dawn. Then he looked at Wren’s clenched hand, at Tomas’s frightened face, at Elsbet’s pen lying like a little barred gate across the page.

“Tell Master Kest,” he called, voice shaking but audible, “that Saint Brenna’s copy is not yet fair.”

Silence answered. Then the boy said, uncertain, “He said not to send twice.”

Elsbet went to the door before Orlan could take the warning back. She opened it only a hand’s width. A manor errand boy stood on the step with rain running down his nose and Ruan’s impatience borrowed badly on his face.

“You have your answer,” she said.

“He will ask who delayed it.”

Elsbet felt Tomas behind her. She felt Wren. She felt the weight of the rough book and the cord and every clean place where a lie liked to sit.

“Elsbet Vale,” she said. “Copyist.”

She shut the door before courage could reconsider.

Wren had not moved when Elsbet turned back. The cord hung from her fist, blue and yellow darkened by rain.

“Does that save her?” Wren asked.

“No.” Elsbet picked up her pen. Her hand shook, so she held it tighter. “It only keeps her from being erased before we find who else is brave enough to remember.”

Wren nodded once, as if one night were not mercy but material.

“Matra Voss remembers candles,” she said.

“Then at first light,” Elsbet said, “we ask what wax can carry.”
