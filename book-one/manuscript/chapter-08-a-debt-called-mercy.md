# Chapter 8 — A Debt Called Mercy

The court had begun in the way a trap began: not by closing, but by inviting people to stand where the closing would seem their own fault.

The manor court house had once been a granary. It still smelled of old barley under the wet wool, damp leather, river mud, and hot rushes smoking in wall brackets. Benches filled both sides of the room. Farmers held caps against their knees. Two wool men argued in whispers over a torn sack. A woman with a goose in a basket kept shushing the goose as if the court might fine birds for opinion. At the far end, beneath Lady Merrow's faded hawk banner, the steward's chair sat empty on a low dais.

Not absent. Waiting. That was worse.

A clerk stood below the dais reading small matters in a voice made thin by hurry. Washed fence. Lame horse. Broken bridge plank. Ferry delay. Each complaint slid into the next until Elsbet could hear how Ruan liked the world: trouble reduced to lines, lines reduced to fees, fees reduced to obedience.

Ruan Kest did not raise his voice when they entered. He did not need to. His dry coat and clean boots did the shouting.

"Father Orlan," he said, "the register table is there. Keep your saint's book wrapped until called. Mistress Vale, a word before you waste the steward's hearing. The child can wait by the hearth."

"The child has a name," Wren said.

Several heads turned. Not many. Most people had learned the cost of looking interested.

Ruan's smile did not move above his mouth. "Names are why we are here. By the hearth."

Wren's fingers tightened around the blue and yellow cord wound at her wrist. Father Orlan shifted the rough register under his cloak as if the book had grown heavier on the walk. The broad manor man touched the bell handle at his belt.

Elsbet stepped between that hand and Wren before she had decided to. "If the court means to discuss Wren's placement, she should hear where people want to put her."

"The court means first to discover why Saint Brenna's fair copy is not fair." Ruan turned slightly, opening a space beside a side table where a lamp burned low and three clean sheets lay weighted by a knife. "That is copyist business. Unless you need the girl to tell you what your own hand has written."

Father Orlan said, "I should be present."

"You will be present when called, Father." Ruan's eyes rested on the register cloth. "Unless you prefer the steward begin by asking why a priest carried an unsealed quarrel through half the village rain."

The old man swallowed. He looked at Elsbet. Shame, fear, and apology crowded his face and helped no one.

"I will not write out of sight," Elsbet said.

"You will not write at all unless you learn the shape of mercy before Master Brey sits." Ruan's voice stayed mild. "Come. One table. Twenty steps. Every gossip in Harrowick Ford may count your breath if it comforts you."

That was how he made cages: wide enough to call them open.

Elsbet followed him to the side table because refusal there would look like panic, and panic was a poor witness. She kept the unsealed fair copy folded under her arm. She kept Tomas's bond strip in her sleeve. She kept her hands visible.

Ruan placed himself between her and the room, not fully blocking it. A thoughtful cruelty. She could see Wren by the hearth, see Orlan standing near the register table, see the steward's empty chair. She could not reach any of them without brushing past Ruan's sleeve.

He set one of the clean sheets before her.

"No one is asking you to lie," he said.

Elsbet almost laughed. It would have sounded wild, so she did not.

"No," Ruan continued, as if she had agreed. "A lie is a hard thing. It cracks under oath. Uncertainty bends. A crowded marriage line. A priest with poor eyes. A dead ferryman gone beyond summons. A child with a cord no adult kept under seal. A candle mark that could mean household, charity, or habit. A copy slat cut this morning by a frightened widow. You see? I am not cruel enough to demand certainty where God gave us mud."

"God did not write your manor wording."

"A clerk wrote it. Clerks write what can stand." He slid the weighted knife aside and turned the clean sheet so she could read the lines already drafted in another hand.

Maura Reed, called Dain in local use. Winter fever. One minor child, Wren Reed. Marriage entry disputed for want of clear witness. Burial candle count to be noted without prejudice. Meadow and ferry to remain under manor handling pending harvest review.

Elsbet read it once. Then again. The words were not clumsy. That was their danger.

No husband entered had been a theft with its fist showing. This was softer. This gave Wren a review. This kept the ninth candle from vanishing. This let Ruan hold the meadow only pending harvest, which in flood season meant holding it when it mattered. This did not erase Maura entirely. It only loosened the knot until the cord could fall off by itself.

"No," Elsbet said.

"You answer too quickly for a woman with a brother." Ruan drew a second paper from his coat. Mill twine. Brown wax. A copy of Tomas's bond, drier and cleaner than the strip in her sleeve. "Miller Dannet has asked whether late payment and household misconduct permit full labor binding until Lammas. I advised him to wait. I could advise longer."

The court clerk's thin voice carried from the dais. "Moat Lane timber charge, two pence per loaded cart until repair."

Somewhere behind Elsbet, a man cursed under his breath. The goose complained softly in its basket.

Ruan tapped Tomas's bond with one clean fingernail. "Your brother has strong shoulders and little sense. Men like that become other men's tools if no one speaks carefully for them. Sign a careful uncertainty, and his dates become forgiving. Father Orlan's chapel debt can wait until Michaelmas. The girl eats under a roof tonight, not in a cart yard. Matra Voss keeps her candle contract. The meadow is reviewed when roads are dry and tempers are cooler."

"And you hold the ferry through the flood."

"Someone must. Would you prefer no one cross? Grain spoils beautifully while righteous people argue."

He was not wrong enough. Elsbet hated him for that most.

Across the room, Wren stood too close to the hearth and did not warm herself. Father Orlan had laid the wrapped register on the table but kept both hands on it. His shoulders bent over the book like a poor roof. The grain woman from the bend had come in after them; she lingered near the door with her donkey's torn sack folded in her arms, eyes lowered. Not gone. Not speaking. If called.

If called, if sealed, if reviewed, if delayed. Harrowick Ford was built from such small hinges. Poor people lived or broke according to which way they swung.

Ruan placed a pen beside the clean sheet. Not Elsbet's pen. A court pen, trimmed neat, with no old ink bitten into the quill.

"Write it in your hand," he said. "Say the chapel copyist cannot fairly enter Maura Dain as lawful wife without further witness, but recognizes local use. That is not betrayal. It is mercy measured to what the room can bear."

Elsbet looked at the pen.

Tomas at the mill before dawn, lifting sacks with flour on his cheek. Tomas asking if truth was worth his bond. Tomas offering another month of night grinding because he still believed bodies could pay what words owed. Father Orlan's debt paper under seal. Matra's scarred wrist. Wren's open cottage chest. All of them set on one side of a scale.

On the other side: a dead woman's married name, brown ink in a cramped line, and a child who knew wool on knives.

Elsbet picked up the pen.

Ruan let out no breath. That was how she knew he had been holding one.

The quill felt too smooth. Court tools were made for hands that expected obedience from paper. She dipped it, and black gathered at the nib, glossy and ready.

Wren crossed the room.

No one stopped her because no one expected a child to move through silence like a thrown stone. The broad manor man reached too late. Father Orlan said her name, but softly, as if he feared breaking whatever courage had found her.

Wren came to the side table and unwound the cord from her wrist. The blue and yellow strands had darkened with rain and handling. The singed end left a faint soot mark on her skin.

"If you write almost," she said, "do not use this."

Elsbet's hand stilled above the clean sheet.

Ruan's mouth tightened. "Child, this is not—"

"It is mine." Wren did not look at him. She looked only at Elsbet. "You said it cannot stand alone. I heard you. But if you make my mother almost married, then this is almost a cord. Give it back to me before you do it, so I know what kind of thing I am holding."

The room had quieted. Not fully. Courts never quieted fully; fear always had a whispering edge. But enough people had turned now. Enough eyes saw the cord lying between a court pen and a bailiff's paper.

Ruan lowered his voice. "Mistress Vale, do not let a frightened child spend your brother."

There it was. Plain at last. Not mercy. Purchase.

Elsbet looked at the sheet again. Marriage entry disputed. Without prejudice. Manor handling. Each phrase a little basket for carrying theft dry through rain.

She set the court pen down crosswise over Ruan's draft.

"No private uncertainty," she said.

Ruan's face went still.

"Take care."

"I am." Her voice shook, but it carried farther than she meant. "If the entry is uncertain, let it be uncertain in the hearing. Let Father Orlan read the rough line. Let the ninth candle be counted aloud. Let Wren hold her cord where the steward can see who wants it called almost. Let Matra be called if wood is mocked. Let the ferry mark be named if the meadow is called idle. I will not make your softer lie clean in a corner."

The clerk had stopped reading. The steward's empty chair waited no longer like furniture. It waited like a witness.

Ruan bent close enough that only Elsbet and Wren could hear him clearly.

"By sunset," he said, "your brother's bond will have teeth."

Elsbet thought of Tomas's shoulders. She thought of teeth. She thought of all the clean marks that had bitten poor people because someone had spared a room the ugliness of watching.

"Then call him Tomas Vale when you bite," she said. "Do not make him household conduct."

For the first time since the chapel, Ruan looked less than ready. Only for a heartbeat. Then the steward's door opened behind the dais.

Harl Brey entered in a dry brown robe, narrow-faced and annoyed by weather he had not authorized. His eyes went to the stopped clerk, the turned benches, the cord on the side table, Ruan's crossed draft, and Elsbet with ink on a pen she had refused to use.

"Master Kest," the steward said, "why has my court gone quiet?"

Wren took up the cord, but she did not wind it around her wrist again. She held it open between both hands.

Elsbet lifted the unsealed fair copy from under her arm.

"Because Saint Brenna's copy is not yet fair," she said. "And this room should hear why."
