# Chapter 2 — The Missing Ninth

Father Orlan reached for the register as if a hand had risen from it to strike him.

Elsbet put her palm on the page first.

The old priest stopped with his fingers above the cracked leather cover, blue at the knuckles in the brazier light. Outside, the river bell kept ringing: not drowning-fast, not yet, but steady enough to accuse.

“You heard me,” Elsbet said.

“I heard too much.”

“There is another mismatch.”

“Then it will still be there tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow is court.”

Father Orlan looked toward the chapel door, as if Ruan Kest might have left one ear pressed to the wood. “And tonight is when poor people learn whether a truth can ruin them faster than a lie.”

Elsbet did not move her hand. The death page lay under it. Maura Dain’s line disappeared beneath the side of her thumb, but the number stayed visible: ix, cramped after the candle mark, written in Elsbet’s own hand during the winter fever weeks when she had slept in pieces and smelled smoke even in dreams.

“Nine candles,” she said.

“Burial candles are not a marriage.”

“No. But a ninth is not nothing.”

Father Orlan shut his eyes. Rain threaded down the shutter gaps and gathered on the sill in dark beads. A coal settled in the brazier with a soft red sigh.

Elsbet lifted her hand and read the line aloud because silence had begun doing Ruan’s work for him.

“Maura Dain. Winter fever. Nine lights. One child. Ferry bank.”

“Quietly.”

“She is dead quietly enough.”

That brought his eyes open. Shame passed over his face, then anger, then the tired priest’s habit of folding both away before anyone could ask what they had meant.

“The custom is not law,” he said.

“The court takes chapel death notes when there is no better witness.”

“When they suit the court.”

“When the copy is clean.” Elsbet tapped the unfinished fair sheet with the dry end of her pen. “Master Kest’s wording made her Maura Dain for the fever death, but no husband entered for the ward claim. He kept the name where it cost him nothing and cut it where it cost him meadow.”

Father Orlan lowered himself onto the stool. “You think I do not see that?”

“I think you saw it before he came.”

The words were out before she weighed them. They hung between them uglier than a shout.

The priest did not deny it. He rubbed the key cord at his belt until the brass key knocked softly against the bench.

“I saw a crowd of winter lines,” he said. “I saw smoke, fever, half the village coughing blood into rags. I saw my own hand shake. I told myself I would review the D entries when my eyes cleared.”

“And when did they clear?”

He laughed once. It was not amusement. “You have a cruel tongue when frightened.”

“I learned from men with gentle ones.”

Father Orlan looked at her then, properly. “The ninth candle was paid?”

“Yes.” Elsbet bent closer, though she knew the mark. “Here. A little notch after the ix. Fee short by one farthing. Promised at thaw.”

“Who promised?”

She turned the page back and forward, following cramped fever marks: initials, candle sizes, debt scraps, who fetched the body, who stood close enough to count as kin. The rich bought wax and space; the poor bought one flame at a time and prayed the saint could add.

“Ferry bank,” Elsbet said. “No hand named.”

“Then it is weak.”

“Not empty.”

“A court eats weak things.”

“Then we find its teeth first.”

Father Orlan made the warning sound again, but softer now. Elsbet heard the grief under it and hated that grief could be another locked door.

She pulled the register nearer and set a clean work scrap beside the fair copy. Not lawful, not sealed; only the kind a copyist used to spare good paper. She wrote the death line exactly, then stopped before the candle mark.

“Tell me the custom as you would tell it at court,” she said.

He pressed his hands together like an old man warming them over words he wished would burn.

“Eight lights for any soul buried from Saint Brenna’s door when there is coin or charity enough,” he said at last. “One at the feet, one at the head, two for the watchers, four for the road prayers.”

“And the ninth?”

“The household light.”

Elsbet wrote household light.

“Not always,” he said quickly.

She looked up.

“Do not make that face. I am being exact. The ninth is set when a household claims the dead aloud: husband, wife, house-holding child, sometimes a guild, sometimes the chapel if no one else will stand.”

“But not for a woman the court says had no husband entered.”

“Not for a woman no household claimed.”

Elsbet underlined household, then regretted wasting ink on anger. “Who stood for Maura?”

Father Orlan looked away.

“Father.”

“I remember Wren at the bier.”

“She was eleven.”

“Twelve now. Eleven then.”

“A child cannot claim a household candle.”

“No.”

“Then who?”

The rain answered first. It came harder, flattening itself against the chapel roof. Somewhere beyond the wall, men shouted near the ford, their voices thinned by water and distance.

“Edric Dain’s cord was laid on the coffin,” Father Orlan said.

Elsbet’s pen stilled.

“Not by Edric,” he added. “He was gone by then, wherever the river or road took him. Wren laid it there. Blue and yellow. The same cord from the marriage line, if memory serves, though memory is apparently a poor witness unless it is a bailiff’s.”

“The cord is in Wren’s keeping?”

“It was.”

“Did you note it?”

“No.”

Elsbet stared at him.

“No,” he said again, sharper because he deserved the first one. “I noted the candle. I noted the child. I noted ferry bank because Maura was buried from that house. I did not write every object a grieving child put on a coffin while half the village waited for me to say words over their own dead.”

His voice broke on the last phrase, just a little. Elsbet let the silence take it. Not because she forgave him. Because truth, too, needed room to breathe if it was going to stand upright.

She wrote cord? and circled it once.

Father Orlan saw. “Do not go to the cottage.”

“I wrote it like a question.”

“Questions can trespass.”

“So can wardship men.”

He flinched. Elsbet was ashamed of the satisfaction. Fear had made her mean and clear, and she was beginning to understand why men like Ruan liked clarity.

A knock came at the side door.

Not the hard gate bell. Not Ruan’s iron. This was knuckles on swollen wood, quick and uncertain, then a shoulder against the door when the latch stuck.

Father Orlan grabbed the linen square and covered the register.

Elsbet folded her work scrap under her hand.

The door opened far enough to spill rain and a boy into the chapel.

Tomas Vale was sixteen: nearly a man when the miller wanted sacks lifted, still a child when debt was counted. Rain plastered his fair hair to his forehead. Flour paste streaked one cheek white.

“Els,” he said, trying the smile he always tried first. “Miller sent me down.”

“At this hour?”

“Master Kest wanted the bond mark checked before dusk.”

Father Orlan’s eyes went to Elsbet.

Tomas either missed the look or chose to. He crossed the room dripping onto the flags and held out a folded parchment tied with mill twine, sealed with a thumb of brown wax. Not the manor hawk. Lower than that. Crueler, because it needed no emblem.

Elsbet took it. Her fingers left ink on the damp outside.

“You opened it?” she asked.

Tomas shook his head. “Miller said it was for you to see if you had forgot the dates.”

“I have not forgot.”

“I know.” His smile came again and failed faster. “He said late is late even in flood season.”

Elsbet broke the wax.

The bond inside was copied in the miller’s square hand, but a new line had been added at the bottom in a smoother one.

Review pending at court bell. Household conduct to be considered.

No threat. No demand. Only conduct, a word clean enough to wash blood from its sleeves.

Tomas leaned close, trying to read upside down. He could make out numbers, some names, not the whole of it. Elsbet folded the strip before his shame could ask her for help.

“What does it say?” he asked.

“That Ruan Kest has learned to write without spelling himself a villain.”

Father Orlan whispered, “Elsbet.”

Tomas looked from one to the other. Rainwater ran from his sleeve and tapped steadily on the floor.

“I can work another month,” he said. “If that is all. I told Miller I could take night grinding when the flood grain comes. I do not mind.”

Elsbet wanted to cover his mouth before he offered more of himself to men who measured bodies by debt. Instead she set the bond strip beside Maura’s death line.

Two clean records. Two traps.

“Go home,” she said.

“The lane by the mill is under. I can wait here.”

“You cannot.”

Hurt crossed his face.

She made herself softer. “If Ruan’s men ask where you stood, I need you able to say you stood nowhere near this table.”

His eyes dropped to the covered register, the fair copy, the work scrap half-hidden under her hand. Poverty did not make people stupid. It only made cleverness expensive.

“You found something,” he said.

Father Orlan began, “No one found—”

“A ninth candle,” Elsbet said.

The priest stopped.

Tomas frowned. “For Widow Dain?”

“For Maura Dain.”

“That is what I said.”

“No,” Elsbet said. “It is not.”

He heard the difference then, or enough of it. His face changed in the way faces changed when a door opened onto bad weather.

“Is it worth my bond?” he asked.

There it was, plain as a struck bell. Not cowardice. Arithmetic.

Elsbet had no answer clean enough for a brother.

Before she could make a dirty one, the front latch lifted.

All three of them turned.

The chapel door opened against the rain, slow because the wind pressed it back. A small figure stood in the gap with a shawl pulled over her head and both hands clenched around something hidden beneath the wool.

Wren Dain looked first at Father Orlan, then at Tomas, and last at Elsbet.

“My mother’s name,” she said. “Are you writing it down or rubbing it out?”
