## Full Story Sense Check
The story is about Elsbet Vale, a poor chapel copyist in Harrowick Ford, refusing to let a bailiff turn a dead woman's marriage and a child's inheritance into a clean omission. The protagonist is Elsbet. The central human conflict is not magic or grand politics, but whether local institutions—chapel register, flood court, ferry tolls, debts, candle customs, and witness habits—can be pressured into spending Wren Dain so Ruan Kest can control Nine-Candle Meadow and the flood ferry. The emotional spine is Elsbet learning that accurate copying is not neutral when powerful men ask her to make absence look lawful, while Wren learns that partial safety is still worth fighting for when erasure is the alternative.

Elsbet wants to keep the fair copy from becoming a weapon against Wren without sacrificing Tomas, Father Orlan, Matra, or the chapel seal. From beginning to end, she changes from a copyist trying to survive inside rules into someone willing to make the rules speak publicly, even when the result is only delay and risk. Chapters 1–3 are essential because they establish access, threat, Wren's voice, and the choice not to seal. Chapters 4–7 are essential but can occasionally linger on procedure and travel; they gather the slat, meadow, custody, and Glass Mile route pressure. Chapters 8–12 are the active court-and-margin sequence. Chapter 10 is essential but currently the longest and most repeated in explaining that the outcome is partial, so it is the best compression target. The ending pays off the beginning by returning to the fair copy and turning the ordered absence into a margin cause. The middle makes that ending more inevitable through cumulative evidence, custody rules, and public witness pressure. No chapter is making the story collapse, but Chapter 10 risks slowing the climax by restating stakes the reader already understands.

## Grounded Logic Check
The plot mechanism makes institutional sense. Elsbet has access because she is appointed chapel copyist; Father Orlan controls the rough register and seal; Ruan controls court timing, debt pressure, and manor wording but cannot simply seize the chapel record without scandal; Matra controls candle slats and risks her contract; Wren has the cord and cottage/ferry knowledge but lacks adult legal standing. Access to each object has rules: rough register under chapel custody, fair copy unsealed, original candle slat with Matra, copy slat and ferry tally under chapel key, cord with Wren, manor draft with Ruan, court roll with Harl's clerk. More powerful people cannot solve it cleanly because Harl is procedural rather than benevolent, Lady Merrow is absent, and Ruan's leverage is local and immediate. Events happen now because flood court, bridge damage, quarterly copying, and ferry economics converge. Characters act from debt, fear, reputation, hunger, custody, grief, and institutional self-protection rather than convenience.
