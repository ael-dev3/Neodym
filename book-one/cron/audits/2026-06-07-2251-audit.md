## Full Story Sense Check

The story is currently about Elsbet Vale, a chapel copyist, refusing to make a clean false fair copy that would erase Maura Dain's marriage and allow Ruan Kest to take Nine-Candle Meadow and its ferry control before flood court. The protagonist is Elsbet; the central human conflict is whether a poor functionary will spend another poor child to protect her brother, priest, and village institutions from a bailiff's procedural pressure. The emotional spine runs from Elsbet treating records as work, to understanding that clean paper can carry theft, to choosing a public, limited truth even when it exposes Tomas.

Elsbet wants first to finish lawful work and keep her household safe; by the end she wants Maura Dain's name to survive in a form that cannot pretend the knife was never there. What changes from beginning to end is not the whole legal world but the public record: absence fails to become clean law, Wren is not handed to the manor, the ferry stays under old tolls temporarily, and Maura's disputed name enters the margin.

Essential chapters: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, and 12. Chapter 2 is essential but could still be lightly tightened. Chapter 7 is now useful as route pressure after prior revision. Chapter 9 is essential but feels most like connective court machinery. The ending pays off the beginning because the fair copy introduced as dangerous clean paper becomes the site of Elsbet's limited moral action. The middle makes the ending more inevitable by building the proof chain and showing why no single object can save Wren. No chapter currently makes the story make less sense, but Chapter 9 can make the story feel slower than it is.

## Grounded Logic Check

The plot mechanism still makes institutional sense. Elsbet has access because she is the appointed chapel copyist handling the fair copy. Father Orlan controls the rough register and chapel key; Ruan controls timing, debt pressure, and manor-facing language; Harl Brey controls the court roll but cares about appealable dirt more than justice. Wren can bring her own cord and tally board but cannot make them decisive. Matra controls her private slats and risks her contract if she speaks.

Important access rules remain plausible: rough register under chapel custody, fair copy in Elsbet's assigned hand, manor chest inaccessible, candle slats in Matra's shed, household cord with Wren, ferry marks visible but not decisive. Elsbet's involvement is believable because her hand and witness mark make the false copy lawful. More powerful people cannot solve this cleanly because Lady Merrow is absent, Harl is form-first, Father Orlan is compromised, and Ruan's leverage is distributed through ordinary debts. If Elsbet is caught overreaching, Tomas, Orlan, Matra, and Wren all pay.

No current “why would this happen?” failure breaks the draft. The court scenes are logically strong, but Chapter 9 lists the proof chain in a way that can feel like notes being arranged for the reader rather than pressure happening to people.
