## Full Story Sense Check
The story is currently about Elsbet Vale, Saint Brenna's chapel copyist, refusing to turn a lawful fair copy into a quiet erasure of Maura Dain's marriage and Wren Dain's ferry claim. Elsbet is the protagonist. The central human conflict is not magic or destiny but whether a low-born record keeper can make a powerful bailiff's theft visible before debt, court form, and village need make it seem clean. The emotional spine is Elsbet learning that keeping one child from being spent by fear may put her own brother's bond at risk, while Wren learns that adults can help only in fractions and must still be held to account.

Elsbet wants to avoid signing a false fair copy and to keep her household safe. From beginning to end she changes from a careful copyist hoping procedure will protect her into a named witness who accepts that truth must be made public and costly if it is to stand. Essential chapters are 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 11, and 12; Chapters 2, 4, 6, 7, and 9 carry necessary proof-chain and institution pressure but remain the chapters most likely to feel procedural if their language slackens. The ending pays off the beginning by returning to the fair copy and writing Maura Dain into the margin where Ruan wanted absence, though it deliberately resolves only the immediate erasure rather than the entire legal future. The middle makes the ending more inevitable by stacking corroborating but limited proof: register, ninth candle, cord, slat, ferry marks, witnesses, court order. No active chapter currently makes the story less coherent, but Chapter 1 can still earn its place harder by making the opening pressure sharper and less explanatory.

## Grounded Logic Check
The plot mechanism makes institutional sense. Elsbet has access because she is the appointed chapel copyist preparing the quarterly fair copy; Father Orlan controls the rough register and seal; Ruan cannot simply steal the chapel book because public matching and court form matter; Harl Brey can delay but not magically deliver justice; Wren has personal custody of the cord and household tally but not adult legal standing; Matra owns the original slat and risks her contract if she speaks too freely. Documents and objects have plausible access rules. The protagonist's involvement is believable because her hand and witness mark make the fair copy lawful, and Ruan's pressure on Tomas gives her a personal cost. Institutions behave with realistic self-protection: chapel debt, stewardly form, manor leverage, trade contracts, and villagers' ferry needs all pull against clean justice. Events are causally connected through flood timing, road failure, ferry value, court deadlines, record custody, and debt pressure. The main "why would this happen now?" answer still works: flood court, damaged bridge approach, quarterly copy deadline, and ferry value converge.
