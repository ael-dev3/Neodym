## Full Story Sense Check
The story is currently about Elsbet Vale, a poor chapel copyist, refusing to make a clean fair copy that would erase Maura Dain's marriage and place Wren's meadow, ferry, and body under manor control. Elsbet is the protagonist. The central human conflict is not magic or empire but a local document fight: a bailiff uses debt, road urgency, wardship custom, and procedural uncertainty to steal a child's inheritance while making the theft look administrative. The emotional spine is Elsbet learning that saving Wren's name may endanger her brother Tomas and still choosing not to let fear choose every word.

Elsbet wants to keep the record truthful without destroying the people who depend on her. From beginning to end she changes from a careful copyist trying to survive inside rules into a named witness who uses the rules publicly against Ruan's private pressure. Essential chapters are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, and 12. Chapter 7 is useful because the Glass Mile acts as route pressure and witness-gathering rather than lore, but it remains the easiest place for the novella to feel wider than needed. Chapter 9 is functional setup for the formal hearing. Chapter 10 is the longest active chapter and repeats several procedural beats that were already established in Chapters 8 and 9; it is the best compression target.

The ending pays off the beginning: the fair copy that looked innocent in Chapter 1 receives a margin cause in Chapter 12 instead of a false omission. The middle makes the ending more inevitable by gathering proof sources and witnesses in a plausible chain: rough register, cord, candle slat, ferry tally, route mark, court order. No active chapter currently makes the story less coherent, but the court sequence can be sharper and should not over-explain what the reader already understands.

## Grounded Logic Check
The plot mechanism makes institutional sense. The rough register stays under chapel custody; the fair copy is Elsbet's job; the manor draft is Ruan's procedural pressure; Matra controls the original candle slat; Wren controls the cord; the ferry/meadow matter because floodwater makes the crossing economically urgent; Harl Brey can delay, order production of material marks, and protect his roll from appeal, but he cannot simply grant a happy ending. Elsbet has believable involvement because her hand makes the fair copy lawful and her refusal creates the form problem. More powerful people do not solve it because Lady Merrow is absent, Harl is procedure-first, Orlan is indebted and afraid, and witnesses depend on summons and contracts. Characters act from motive: Ruan wants controllable toll income and clean accounts, Orlan wants to protect the chapel seal, Matra wants to keep her contract, Tomas wants to avoid bond breach, Wren wants her mother's name and home, and Elsbet wants truth without sacrificing her brother. No major “why would this happen now?” failure remains: flood, moved court, quarterly copy, ferry value, and account pressure all converge.

## Small-Scale Fantasy Check
This feels like a small human novella: chapel, register, ferry, market road, manor court, mill, candle shed, cottage, and flood are the active world. The stakes are meaningful but local: a child's name, a meadow, a ferry lease, a brother's bond, a chapel seal, and village road access. There are not too many factions; the main pressures are chapel, manor, court, trade, and household debt. The story feels much closer to a local road/village/court dispute than an epic saga.
