# 00 — Book One Premise

## Working title

Primary working title: **The Bell on Starvel Hill**

Other title options:

1. **The Bell on Starvel Hill**
2. **The Road That Hummed Back**
3. **The Child the Ledger Named**
4. **The Saint Orra Bridge Case**
5. **A Song Below the Silver Road**
6. **The Toll of Low Wicker**

## One-sentence pitch

A low-born village letter-reader must save an orphaned bell-runner from being taken under unreadable silver law after an old bridge song wakes a distant Aurelith beacon in their muddy sheep valley.

## Back-cover-style premise

Low Wicker is too small to matter to the powers above the hills. Its people keep sheep, bake barley loaves, argue over bridge tolls, bury their dead in wet wool, and step off the road when sealed officials pass.

Nera Brindle earns bread by reading letters for people who cannot read, marking toll slips at Saint Orra's bridge, and helping her aunt at the village oven. She knows the local songs, the old debts, the safe lies, and the exact way to bow before a clerk without seeming proud.

On market morning, twelve-year-old Pell Quick sings the bridge-bell rhyme at the wrong moment. A blue-silver beacon on forbidden Starvel Hill answers him. The mirror-pillar in the moot-house turns black. By sundown, Pell's Name-Iron has marked him for correction under a law no one in Low Wicker is allowed to read.

The road closes. The mill stops. The sheep fair curdles into accusation. Nera has three days to prove the song is an old lawful custom, not forbidden human magic. Her only tools are a half-burned chapel register, a Cairnkin stone-mender everyone mistrusts, a chapel sister who knows more than she can safely say, and her own stubborn memory of a song her mother told her never to sing near silver light.

The Aurelith do not explain themselves. They do not need to. Their beacon hums on the hill, their edict arrives under a human clerk's shaking voice, and everyone in the valley knows that a child can disappear into mercy and never come home.

Nera can save Pell by exposing a local lie, but the truth may cost the village its oldest custom, its road, and the little room humans have left to remember themselves.

## Core local story engine

**One central plot engine:** A village child is marked by silver law after singing an old bridge song near a forbidden Aurelith beacon, and the community must decide whether to surrender him, hide him, or risk proving the custom lawful in a minor human court that can still be overruled.

This is not a rebellion story. It is a local legal, social, and moral dispute inside one rural valley.

## Core local conflict

Low Wicker's human bridge custom requires a bell-rhyme to be sung when sheep, children, or funeral carts cross Saint Orra's bridge. The rhyme is old, practical, and half-superstitious. It also conflicts with a newer silver-law restriction: no unmeasured human song may be raised within hearing of Starvel Hill.

When Pell sings during market toll, the Aurelith beacon answers. Human authorities must produce a lawful explanation within three days or deliver Pell to the hill road for correction. The valley's economy depends on reopening the bridge, so ordinary neighbors begin asking whether one child is worth everyone's winter bread.

## Secret beneath the conflict

The bridge song once marked the true safe boundary around Starvel Hill. It was not made to challenge Aurelith power; it was a human warning song created after earlier children vanished for crossing an invisible line.

Years ago, a flood shifted the riverbank and the toll road. The human court quietly moved the bridge ledger to collect easier tolls, but no one was permitted to alter the silver boundary record. Pell sang at the old song's true place, not the clerk's profitable place. The song did exactly what it was meant to do: warn the valley that the legal road and the forbidden hill-boundary no longer match.

The local antagonist has hidden this mismatch because admitting it would ruin his family, close the bridge through winter, and expose years of illegal toll-taking.

## Moral dilemma

Nera can save Pell by proving that the village song preserves an older truth than the current bridge ledger. But if she proves the song matters, the higher law may ban it entirely, close the road, punish the chapel, and mark more children for knowing words they were never supposed to understand.

If she stays silent, Pell is taken and the village keeps eating.

## Ending promise

The ending should win one human-sized thing:

- Pell is not taken up the silver road.
- The bridge song survives, but in altered, more dangerous form.
- The false toll record is corrected locally.
- Nera becomes the keeper of a story that cannot be safely written in full.

The world remains unjust. Starvel Hill remains forbidden. The Aurelith remain distant. Low Wicker keeps living under rules it does not fully know.

## How this introduces Neodym quietly

The novella introduces Neodym through consequences rather than explanation:

- Name-Irons that bruise or cool against skin.
- A mirror-pillar that reacts in court but never explains itself.
- A blue-silver hill beacon that hums without flame.
- Human clerks enforcing laws they cannot read aloud in full.
- Road restrictions, permits, and social humiliation.
- Local fear of being marked, corrected, archived, or renamed.
- Warm human custom pressed against cold immortal order.

The reader learns what humans can learn: enough to fear the system, not enough to master it.
